## In one sentence

The digits 299,792,458 are a human choice about the metre and the second, and the real question, why there is a maximum speed at all, is about the shape of spacetime and not about light.

## Explain it

The speed of light in a vacuum, written as c, is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. Not roughly, exactly, and the reason it is exact is the first clue. Since October 1983, the metre is defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. So if tomorrow someone measured light going faster, the metre would get longer and the number would stay the same. In Layman's terms, we did not discover that c is 299,792,458, we decided that 299,792,458 is what a metre is.

Where do the digits come from, then? From the Earth. The metre was defined in 1793 as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator through Paris, and the second is 1/86,400 of one spin of the planet. Therefore the number 299,792,458 is really "how many ten-millionths of a quarter of the Earth's circumference light covers in one 86,400th of a day", which is a sentence about our planet, not about light. To illustrate, light does about 7.5 laps around the equator in one second, and that 7.5 is just as much a fact about how big the Earth is as it is about light. If we lived on Jupiter and used Jupiter-metres, c would be a different number and nothing in physics would change.

Firstly, though, why does light have a speed at all, and why this one? James Clerk Maxwell worked it out in 1865. His equations for electricity and magnetism predicted a wave whereby a changing electric field creates a magnetic field, which creates an electric field, and so on, and the speed of that wave came out as 1 divided by the square root of two constants of empty space, the permittivity (how much the vacuum resists forming an electric field) and the permeability (the same for a magnetic field). When he put the numbers in, he got roughly 310,000 km/s, which was so close to the measured speed of light that he concluded light must be that wave. Truth be told, that is one of the great moments in physics, but it only moves the question. Why are those two constants what they are? And the honest answer is that they too are just conversion factors set by our choice of units for charge and current.

Secondly, Einstein in 1905 turned the whole thing upside down. The speed c is not really about light. It is the one speed that every observer measures the same no matter how fast they are moving, and that only works if space and time are linked into one thing, spacetime, where c is the exchange rate between metres and seconds, the same way 1.609 is the exchange rate between miles and kilometres. Anything with zero mass has to travel at exactly that speed, so light does, and so do gravitational waves, and so do gluons inside a proton. Light just happens to be the massless thing we noticed first, so the speed got its name. Physicists who want to be precise call it the speed of causality, meaning the fastest that any cause can reach any effect. In the units physicists actually use for this work, c is simply 1, and metres and seconds are the same unit.

So why is there a maximum speed at all, as opposed to none? This is where I hit my gap. The best answer I found is that a universe with no speed limit would have no separation between cause and effect, since everything could affect everything else instantly, and the geometry of our spacetime simply comes with a finite c built in. Nobody has a deeper reason for it than "that is the kind of universe we are in". Thirdly, if you want a number that is not a human artefact, you have to ask about a dimensionless one, meaning a number with no units at all. The famous one is the fine-structure constant, alpha, which is about 1/137.036 and is built out of c, the electron's charge, Planck's constant and the permittivity of space in such a way that all the units cancel. If c changed but alpha stayed the same, no experiment could tell. If alpha changed, chemistry would change. Richard Feynman called 1/137 one of the greatest mysteries of physics, and as of 2026 nobody knows why it has that value. That is the actual "why" question, and it is still open.

Lastly, how did we ever get the number in the first place? Ole Rømer in 1676 noticed that the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io ran late when the Earth was far from Jupiter, and worked out that light must take about 22 minutes to cross the Earth's orbit, which gave roughly 220,000 km/s. Hippolyte Fizeau in 1849 shone light through a spinning toothed wheel and got 313,000 km/s. Albert Michelson spent his whole career on it and by 1926 had 299,796 km/s. By 1975 the number was known to 4 parts in a billion, which was more precise than the old metre itself, so in 1983 the metre was redefined and the measuring stopped.

## Example / non-example

- Example: GPS. The satellites orbit at 14,000 km/h and sit higher in Earth's gravity, so their clocks run about 38 microseconds a day fast relative to ours. At c, 38 microseconds is about 11 km of position error a day, which is why every GPS satellite carries the relativity correction and your Grab driver finds you.
- Not an example: light in glass or water. In glass, light travels at about two-thirds of c, but that is not c changing, it is light being absorbed and re-emitted by atoms along the way. The speed limit is still c, and in fact electrons in a nuclear reactor's cooling water go faster than light-in-water (never faster than c), which is why the water glows blue. That is Cherenkov radiation.

## Why it matters

- It changes what question I ask. "Why is c 299,792,458" has a boring answer (the French and the Earth). "Why is alpha 1/137" has no answer, and that is the interesting one.
- It decides that nothing, no signal, no information, no spaceship, will ever cross the 4.2 light years to Proxima Centauri in under 4.2 years, which puts a hard ceiling on the whole of human ambition.
- It is the reason E = mc squared has a c in it: mass is energy measured in the wrong units, and c squared is just the exchange rate.
- Met it in: SPM physics, where c was a number to plug into a formula, and then this week when I finally asked why it was that number and found out I had been asking the wrong question.
