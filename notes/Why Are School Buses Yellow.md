## In one sentence

School buses across the United States and several other countries are painted a very specific shade, "National School Bus Glossy Yellow," because a 1939 national conference of transportation experts deliberately tested and chose the exact colour that remains most visible to the human eye in both bright daylight and the low light of early morning and dusk, precisely when children are most often walking to and from school.

## Explain it

Truth be told, before 1939, school buses across different American states were painted in a genuinely inconsistent range of colours, making it harder for other drivers to instantly recognise a school bus and react appropriately around it. Frank Cyr, a professor at Columbia University's Teachers College, organised a national conference specifically to standardise school transportation, and the assembled experts tested a range of yellow shades against other colours specifically for visibility under low-light conditions, ultimately selecting the particular yellow-orange shade that studies showed catches peripheral human vision more effectively than most other colours, especially in dim light.

This in turn is exactly why that specific shade, rather than any generic yellow, became the mandated national standard, human peripheral vision, the area outside the direct point of focus, responds especially strongly to yellow wavelengths, making a bus painted this colour noticeably easier to spot out of the corner of an eye than a bus in most other colours, a genuinely meaningful safety advantage during the exact low-light hours many children travel to and from school. In addition, this same colour standard was later adopted by several other countries beyond the United States specifically because the underlying visibility research applies universally rather than being tied to any particular national context.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a driver glancing away briefly and then quickly noticing a stopped school bus out of the corner of their eye is a direct real-world demonstration of the peripheral vision advantage the 1939 conference specifically selected this yellow shade to exploit.
- Non-example: city transit buses are typically painted in whatever colours suit a transit agency's branding, since the specific 1939 safety research and resulting standardisation applied only to school transportation, not to public buses generally, which face a genuinely different set of safety and visibility priorities.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond an interesting standardisation story? Firstly, this colour choice reflects a broader pattern in safety design generally, choosing colours and designs based on measured human perception research rather than aesthetic preference alone, a principle also behind the choice of red for stop signals covered in this vault's own [[Why Are Traffic Lights Red Yellow and Green]] note.

In addition, the 1939 conference did not stop at colour alone, it also standardised bus dimensions and design features still largely in use today, establishing an early and genuinely influential template for how safety standardisation across an entire industry can be achieved through coordinated expert agreement rather than leaving each operator to make its own separate choice.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that this specific yellow was never chosen for tradition or visual appeal, it was chosen through deliberate visibility testing, a rare case where a now-ubiquitous design choice actually has a documented, measurable safety rationale behind it.
