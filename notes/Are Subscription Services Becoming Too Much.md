## My take in one line

Individual subscriptions are each a genuinely reasonable value, but the shift of entire product categories, software, streaming, even cars, from one-time purchase to recurring payment has quietly and collectively raised how much people pay for the same things they used to buy once, and most people have not actually totalled up what that costs them.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I only noticed how far this had gone when I sat down and actually listed every recurring charge hitting my card in a month, streaming, cloud storage, a fitness app, software, and the total surprised me far more than any single subscription would have on its own.

## The case for

Firstly, subscription pricing genuinely lowers the barrier to trying something, a five to fifteen dollar monthly fee is a far smaller decision than a one-time fifty or hundred dollar purchase, which is a real, legitimate benefit for products people are unsure they will use long term. In addition, subscriptions fund continuous improvement in a way one-time purchases structurally cannot, software in particular benefits from an ongoing revenue stream that pays for updates, security patches and new features long after the initial sale, rather than the older model where a company's incentive to keep improving a product mostly ended once it had your money.

Furthermore, subscriptions let companies smooth out revenue and plan investment more predictably than relying on lumpy one-time sales, a genuine business benefit that, at least in theory, can translate into better long-term product support for the customer too.

## The case against

On the other hand, the sheer number of separate subscriptions an average household now carries has grown enough that most people meaningfully underestimate their own total monthly spend, since each individual charge feels small in isolation, a psychological effect researchers call payment decoupling, where the pain of paying is spread thin enough across many small transactions to barely register compared to one larger, more noticeable purchase.

Moreover, the shift has expanded well beyond software and entertainment into categories where it fits far less naturally, heated seats sold as a monthly car subscription being a widely mocked example, and where a feature the hardware already physically supports gets locked behind a recurring fee purely because the manufacturer can now meter access to it after the sale, a possibility one-time-purchase products never offered.

## Where I land

In my opinion, the subscription model is a reasonable fit for genuinely ongoing services, streaming content that keeps being added, software that keeps being maintained, and a poor, extractive fit for features a piece of hardware already has built in. In our case, the exercise of actually totalling my own subscriptions was worth doing regardless of where anyone lands on the broader argument, since the honest total is almost always higher than the felt cost of paying for each one separately.

(To which a friend cancelling three subscriptions after doing the same exercise put it simply, "I wasn't paying attention to the total, only the parts," which is exactly the decoupling effect at work.)

## What would change my mind

If subscription fatigue produced a sustained, measurable consumer backlash, bundling services back together or reverting hardware features to one-time purchase, that would suggest the market is self-correcting the extractive cases faster than I currently expect.
