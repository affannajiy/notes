## In one sentence

Space exploration has shifted over the past two decades from an almost exclusively government-run activity into one increasingly driven by private companies, and that shift has come down mostly to one specific engineering achievement, reusable rockets, which cut launch costs dramatically compared to the fully disposable rockets that dominated spaceflight for its first half century.

## What it is

Space exploration covers the scientific study and physical exploration of outer space, using satellites, robotic probes and human spaceflight, and depends fundamentally on rocket technology capable of reaching orbital velocity, roughly 28,000 kilometres per hour, fast enough to continuously fall around the Earth rather than back down onto it.

## Types

Uncrewed missions, satellites, planetary rovers and deep space probes, make up the large majority of active space activity, since they are far cheaper and lower risk than crewed missions while still delivering most of the scientific data space agencies actually need. Crewed spaceflight, orbital missions to the International Space Station and, increasingly, private space tourism flights, remains dramatically more expensive and complex, given the additional life support, safety margins and return requirements a human crew demands compared to robotic hardware.

## Terms

A few terms recur across space discussion worth defining plainly. "Reusable rocket," a rocket booster capable of landing intact and flying again rather than being discarded after a single launch, is the innovation most directly credited with cutting launch costs, since manufacturing an entirely new rocket for every mission had long been one of spaceflight's largest expenses. "Low Earth orbit," the region up to about 2,000 kilometres above Earth where the International Space Station and most communication satellites operate, is distinct from geostationary orbit, roughly 36,000 kilometres up, where a satellite's orbital period matches Earth's rotation, letting it stay fixed over one point on the ground.

## History in moments

The Soviet Union's launch of Sputnik in 1957 marked the start of the space age, triggering the Cold War-driven space race that culminated in the United States' Apollo 11 crewed Moon landing in 1969. Space activity slowed in ambition, though not in volume, through the following decades, dominated by satellite deployment and space station operations rather than further crewed deep space missions. SpaceX's first successful landing and reuse of an orbital rocket booster in 2015 and 2017 respectively marked a genuine turning point, proving reusability was commercially viable and setting off a wave of private investment and competition in launch services that continues accelerating today.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia operates its own satellites through national space agencies and telecommunications companies, and the country has expressed interest in developing a stronger domestic space industry, though it remains a comparatively small player globally next to the major state and private programmes.

## What's happening now

Private companies now compete directly for both government and commercial launch contracts, and renewed interest in crewed lunar missions, alongside early-stage plans for eventual crewed Mars missions, has revived a level of public attention to space exploration not seen consistently since the original Apollo era.
