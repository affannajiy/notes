## In one sentence

A percentage on its own hides the base number it was calculated from, so a genuinely huge relative change, "risk doubled", can describe a shift from one case to two, while a modest-sounding relative change can sit on top of millions, meaning the same word "increase" can describe wildly different real-world magnitudes depending on what you started counting from.

## Explain it

Truth be told, percentages are useful precisely because they strip away scale, letting you compare a small company's growth rate to a giant's on equal footing, but that same stripping-away is exactly what makes them easy to use misleadingly. A headline claiming a product's sales "grew 200 percent" sounds dramatic, and it might be, but if last year's sales were ten units and this year's are thirty, the absolute change is twenty units, a figure that would draw far less attention printed plainly.

This in turn is why percentage points and percentages themselves get confused so often, if a tax rate rises from 10 percent to 15 percent, that is a five percentage point increase, but described as a percentage increase relative to the original rate, it is actually a 50 percent increase, and journalists, marketers and sometimes researchers pick whichever framing produces the more attention-grabbing number rather than the more accurate one. In addition, percentages calculated from a small base number swing wildly with tiny absolute changes, a disease affecting two people out of a hundred rising to four people out of a hundred is technically a 100 percent increase in cases, a true statement that still describes only two additional people.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a news report stating a rare side effect "doubled in risk" after a new drug's approval sounds alarming, until the actual figures show the risk moved from one in a million to two in a million, a change that remains vanishingly small in absolute terms despite the accurate relative doubling.
- Non-example: a company reporting flat 2 percent revenue growth might actually represent several million currency units of real additional revenue if the underlying base is large enough, a case where the unexciting percentage badly undersells the absolute scale of the change.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond spotting a misleading headline? Firstly, this is exactly why careful reporting on health risks, economic figures or company performance pairs the percentage with the absolute numbers behind it, since the percentage alone genuinely cannot be interpreted correctly without knowing the base it was calculated from.

In addition, this gap between relative and absolute framing gets deliberately exploited in marketing and political messaging, choosing whichever framing, relative or absolute, sounds more favourable to whatever point is being made, a practice close enough to the base-rate confusion covered in this vault's [[Why Polls Can Be Wrong]] note that both rely on readers not asking "compared to what, exactly."

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is a simple habit, whenever a percentage change is mentioned without the underlying absolute numbers, that is itself worth treating as a signal to ask what the actual starting and ending figures were before deciding how impressed or alarmed to be.
