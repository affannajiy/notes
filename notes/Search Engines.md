## In one sentence

Search engines exist to solve one specific problem, the web has no built-in index or table of contents, and every search engine, regardless of which company built it, still relies on the same three-stage process, crawling, indexing and ranking, covered in this vault's [[How Does Google Search Work]] note, even where the specific algorithms and business models built on top of that process differ sharply.

## What it is

A search engine is a system that discovers, catalogues and ranks content across the web so users can find relevant information through a text query, and while Google dominates the market by a wide margin, competing search engines exist with genuinely different priorities, some on privacy, some on independent indexing versus relying on another engine's underlying results.

## Types

General web search engines, Google, Bing, aim to cover the broadest possible range of queries across every topic. Privacy-focused search engines, DuckDuckGo prominent among them, deliberately avoid tracking individual users' search history or personalising results based on stored personal data, trading some personalisation for a stronger privacy guarantee. Specialised search engines narrow their scope deliberately, academic search engines indexing only scholarly papers, or e-commerce search engines indexing only product listings, optimising ranking specifically for that narrower domain rather than the open web generally.

## Terms

A few terms recur across search engine discussion worth defining plainly. "Search engine optimisation," often abbreviated SEO, describes the practice of structuring a website specifically to rank higher in search results, a practice search engines themselves have to continuously guard against being gamed through low-quality but technically well optimised content. "Featured snippet," a direct answer box shown above standard search results for certain queries, extracts and displays text directly from a source page, a feature that has drawn criticism from some publishers for reducing the need to actually click through to the original site.

## History in moments

Early search engines, AltaVista and Yahoo among them in the mid-1990s, relied heavily on simple keyword matching, ranking pages mainly by how often a search term appeared, a method easily exploited by low-quality pages stuffing keywords to rank artificially high. Google's 1998 launch introduced PageRank, an algorithm ranking pages partly by how many other reputable pages linked to them, treating links as a form of endorsement, a genuinely significant improvement in result quality that helped Google quickly overtake its earlier competitors. More recently, large language models have begun reshaping search interfaces themselves, with AI-generated summary answers increasingly appearing above traditional link results, a shift still actively reshaping how search engines present information as of the mid-2020s.

## Malaysian connection

Google remains the dominant search engine in Malaysia as in most of the world, though local businesses increasingly invest in search engine optimisation specifically targeting Malay and Malaysian English search terms to reach domestic audiences more effectively than generic English-only optimisation would.

## What's happening now

The rise of AI-generated answers embedded directly into search results is prompting real concern among publishers and website owners, since a user who gets a satisfactory answer directly on the search results page has less reason to click through to the original source, potentially undermining the advertising and traffic model many websites have relied on for years.
