## In one sentence

Mythology exists across nearly every culture in history for the same underlying reason, to explain the things ancient people had no scientific means of explaining otherwise, why the sun rises, why storms happen, why humans die, and the specific gods and stories differ wildly by culture even while the underlying human need they answer stays remarkably consistent.

## What it is

Mythology refers to a body of traditional stories, particularly ones involving gods, supernatural beings and legendary events, that a culture uses to explain natural phenomena, establish moral values or account for its own origins, generally passed down orally for generations before ever being written down.

## Types

Creation myths, explaining how the world, humans or a specific people came into existence, appear across essentially every culture studied, the Mesopotamian Enuma Elish and the Greek Theogony both describing an ordered world emerging out of primordial chaos in broadly similar structural terms despite arising independently. Hero myths, following a protagonist through trials that transform them, follow a structure scholar Joseph Campbell called the "hero's journey," a pattern he argued recurs across cultures with little direct contact, from Greek Hercules to stories found across Southeast Asian oral tradition. Trickster myths, featuring a cunning, rule-breaking figure, Loki in Norse mythology or Anansi the spider in West African tradition, serve a genuinely different social function, often testing or subtly criticising a society's own norms through a character who deliberately breaks them.

## Terms

A few terms recur across mythology discussion worth defining plainly. "Pantheon," the full collective set of gods and deities recognised within a particular mythological tradition, varies enormously in size and structure, some cultures organising theirs into a single strict hierarchy, others treating deities as more loosely related and equal in status. "Syncretism," the process by which two distinct mythological or religious traditions blend together, often happened when cultures came into extended contact through trade or conquest, Roman mythology absorbing and renaming many Greek gods being a particularly well documented example.

## History in moments

Ancient Greek mythology, systematised in writing by Homer and Hesiod around the 8th century BC, became one of the most extensively documented mythological traditions specifically because Greek literary culture valued written preservation unusually highly for its time. The spread of Norse mythology through Viking expansion across the 8th to 11th centuries carried Scandinavian myths into parts of the British Isles, where fragments blended with local folklore that still surfaces in some regional traditions today. Modern retellings and adaptations, particularly through 20th and 21st century film and literature, have introduced ancient mythological figures to audiences who might otherwise never encounter the original source material at all.

## Malaysian connection

Malay mythology carries its own rich tradition, the legendary warrior Hang Tuah and the origin stories woven into the Sejarah Melayu chronicle blending historical figures with clearly mythological embellishment, a pattern common to many cultures' early historical records before the line between history and myth was drawn as sharply as it is today.

## What's happening now

Mythological figures and stories continue getting reinterpreted through modern film, games and literature, a commercial trend that has renewed genuine public interest in original source myths even as the adaptations themselves often diverge significantly from the ancient texts they draw from.
