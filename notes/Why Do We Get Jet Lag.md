## In one sentence

Jet lag happens because the body's internal clock, the circadian rhythm, is regulated by a small structure in the brain that adjusts to light and darkness only gradually, roughly one time zone per day, so flying across several time zones in a matter of hours leaves that internal clock badly out of sync with the actual local time at the destination.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "jet lag is just tiredness from travelling" undersells what is actually happening, since jet lag can strike even after a comfortable, well-rested flight, which rules out simple fatigue as the real explanation. The body's circadian rhythm is governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a cluster of cells in the brain that uses light exposure, hitting the eyes and processed through a direct neural pathway, as its primary cue for what time it is, and it coordinates the timing of sleep, hormone release, body temperature and digestion around that signal.

This internal clock evolved under conditions where a person's environment and time zone always matched, since no one could physically move fast enough to outrun their own body clock before air travel existed, and it simply was never built to adjust instantly. This in turn means that flying from, say, Kuala Lumpur to London leaves the traveller's internal clock still running on Malaysian time for days after arrival, producing daytime sleepiness, nighttime wakefulness, and disrupted appetite and digestion, all symptoms of internal systems still operating on a schedule that no longer matches the light and darkness actually happening outside.

## Example / non-example

- Example: flying east tends to produce worse jet lag than flying the same number of time zones west, since eastward travel requires the body clock to shift forward, a direction research consistently shows the circadian rhythm adjusts to more slowly than shifting backward, which is what westward travel demands instead.
- Non-example: a long flight within the same or a similar time zone, north to south for instance, produces genuine travel fatigue but not true jet lag, since the destination's light and darkness cycle still roughly matches what the traveller's body clock already expects.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how travellers can manage jet lag in practice? Firstly, this is exactly why deliberate light exposure, seeking bright light at the destination's morning and avoiding it in the destination's evening, is one of the most effective interventions, since light is the primary signal the suprachiasmatic nucleus actually responds to, directly targeting the mechanism causing the mismatch rather than just masking its symptoms.

In addition, this is why gradually shifting sleep and meal times in the days before a long trip, a strategy some frequent long-haul travellers use deliberately, can meaningfully reduce jet lag's severity, it gives the internal clock a head start adjusting before the larger, sudden shift a flight itself causes.

In our case, the detail that reframes jet lag for me is that it is not a flaw in the traveller, it is the body correctly, faithfully doing exactly what it evolved to do, just applied to a situation, instant long-distance travel, that did not exist for nearly all of human evolutionary history.
