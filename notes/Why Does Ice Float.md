## In one sentence

Ice floats because water is one of the rare substances that expands, rather than shrinks, when it freezes, so a fixed mass of ice takes up about 9% more space than the same mass of liquid water, which makes it less dense, and less dense things float on denser ones.

## Explain it

Density is defined as how much mass is packed into a given volume, and it is density, not weight alone, that decides whether something floats. Truth be told, most liquids get denser as they cool, since the molecules slow down and pack closer together, and a solid version of that liquid sinks in its own melt for exactly that reason.

Water breaks the pattern because of the shape of the water molecule itself, two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom in a bent, not straight, shape. This in turn lets water molecules form hydrogen bonds, a weak attraction between the slightly positive hydrogen on one molecule and the slightly negative oxygen on its neighbour. In liquid water these bonds constantly break and reform, letting molecules crowd close together. As water cools past 4 degrees Celsius, whereby that temperature happens to be where liquid water is at its densest, the molecules lock into a fixed hexagonal lattice, meaning a repeating six-sided arrangement, that holds them further apart than they sat as a jostling liquid. The lattice is mostly open space, so the same number of molecules now fills more volume, and density falls.

## Example / non-example

- Example: an ice cube in a glass of water always floats with about 90% of its volume below the surface and 10% above, which is also why icebergs show only the tip above water, the rest hidden underneath.
- Non-example: solid wax sinks in melted wax, and solid iron sinks in molten iron, because both contract on freezing like almost everything else does. Water's expansion on freezing is the unusual case, not the rule.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change anything? Firstly, it is why lakes and rivers freeze from the top down instead of the bottom up. The floating ice layer insulates the water underneath, letting fish and other life survive winter in liquid water below a frozen surface, which would not happen if ice sank and the whole body of water froze solid from the bottom.

In addition, this is the same property behind a burst pipe in winter, whereby water freezing inside a closed pipe expands and cracks the metal or plastic around it, because the ice has nowhere to expand into. And on a larger scale, sea ice floating rather than sinking is part of why the Arctic Ocean has liquid water under its ice cap year round, which matters for [[Why does the sea have tides]] and the wider climate system that depends on it.

In our case, the practical version of this shows up every time I put ice in a drink: it floats, cools the drink from the top as it melts, and the meltwater sinks because liquid water near 0 degrees Celsius is still denser than the ice above it, so the glass keeps stirring itself a little as the cube shrinks.
