## In one sentence

Ridged coin edges, technically called "reeding," were originally introduced centuries ago as a genuine anti-fraud measure, making it immediately visible if someone had shaved small slivers of precious metal off a coin's edge to sell separately, a practical function that has long since become unnecessary but still survives today purely as an easy way to distinguish coin denominations by touch.

## Explain it

Truth be told, coins made from genuinely valuable metals, gold and silver especially, were historically vulnerable to a fraud called "coin clipping," where someone would shave thin slivers off a coin's smooth edge, spend the slightly lighter coin at its full original face value anyway, and then melt down and sell the accumulated shavings separately for additional profit. Isaac Newton, while serving as Warden of the Royal Mint in the late 17th century, was heavily involved in tackling this exact widespread problem, and adding fine ridges around a coin's edge made clipping immediately obvious, since removing even a small sliver would visibly disrupt the previously continuous, evenly spaced pattern.

This in turn is why ridged edges spread widely across precious metal coinage internationally through the following centuries, the fraud-prevention benefit was significant enough that mints across many different countries independently adopted broadly similar reeded-edge designs. In addition, once coins stopped being made from genuinely valuable precious metals, most modern currencies now use cheaper base metal alloys worth far less than their face value, the original clipping-prevention purpose became essentially obsolete, yet the reeded edge persisted anyway, having taken on a genuinely useful secondary purpose, letting visually impaired people and anyone reaching into a pocket or wallet without looking distinguish similarly sized coin denominations purely by feel.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a coin like the American quarter retains ridged edges specifically inherited from an era when quarters actually contained real silver worth roughly their face value, a design feature surviving today purely by convention despite modern quarters containing no precious metal at all.
- Non-example: lower-value coins like the American penny and nickel have always used smooth edges rather than ridges, since these coins were never made from precious metal valuable enough to make clipping fraud a genuine practical concern worth designing against in the first place.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond an interesting bit of currency trivia? Firstly, this history is a genuinely useful example of a design feature outliving its original purpose entirely while still finding a new, practically useful secondary role, a pattern that recurs across many other everyday objects whose original reasoning has simply been forgotten by most people who use them.

In addition, some countries have deliberately continued this reeding tradition specifically for accessibility reasons even on entirely new coin designs, ensuring visually impaired citizens can reliably distinguish denominations by touch alone, a modern justification genuinely separate from the coin's original anti-fraud purpose centuries earlier.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a design feature can genuinely serve two completely different purposes across two different centuries, fraud prevention originally, tactile accessibility today, without the physical design itself ever needing to change at all.
