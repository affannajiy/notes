## In one sentence

Commercial aviation compressed what used to be weeks of travel into hours, and the industry's defining tension has always been the same trade-off, speed and range against the enormous fuel and safety-certification costs that come with pushing either further, a balance that still shapes which aircraft airlines actually order today.

## What it is

An airplane generates lift primarily through wing shape, covered from the physics side in this vault's [[Why Do Planes Stay in the Air]] note, and commercial aviation specifically refers to aircraft operated to carry paying passengers or cargo on scheduled or chartered routes, distinct from military or private general aviation.

## Types

Commercial aircraft split broadly by range and size. Narrow-body aircraft, single-aisle jets like the Boeing 737 and Airbus A320 families, dominate short and medium-haul routes where high frequency matters more than raw capacity. Wide-body aircraft, twin-aisle jets like the Boeing 777 and Airbus A350, handle long-haul intercontinental routes where range and passenger capacity matter more than frequency. A separate, growing category, low-cost carriers, restructures the basic economics of flying by unbundling services, seat selection, baggage, meals, into separate paid add-ons rather than bundling them into one fare, a model AirAsia, covered separately in this vault, applied specifically to Southeast Asia.

## Terms

A few terms recur across aviation discussion worth defining plainly. "Hub-and-spoke," the dominant network model for most major airlines, routes passengers through a small number of central hub airports rather than flying every route directly, trading some extra travel time for far greater route coverage than direct point-to-point flying could support economically. "Wet lease" and "dry lease" describe aircraft leasing arrangements, a wet lease includes crew and maintenance from the leasing company, while a dry lease provides only the aircraft itself, a distinction that matters enormously for how airlines flex capacity up or down without owning every aircraft they operate.

## History in moments

The Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903 proved sustained, controlled flight was possible at all, though decades of further development were needed before aircraft could carry passengers commercially at scale. The introduction of the jet engine into commercial service through the 1950s and 1960s, the Boeing 707 chief among them, cut intercontinental travel time dramatically compared to earlier propeller aircraft, effectively creating the modern era of mass long-haul air travel. Deregulation of airline pricing in the US from 1978 onward, and similar shifts elsewhere later, opened the door to the low-cost carrier model that has since reshaped short-haul travel pricing globally, covered from the crisis side in [[The Boeing 737 MAX Crisis]].

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia is home to AirAsia, one of Southeast Asia's most influential low-cost carriers, and to Malaysia Airlines, the national flag carrier, whose 2014 losses of flights MH370 and MH17 remain among the most significant events in the country's aviation history.

## What's happening now

Airlines and manufacturers face growing pressure to reduce aviation's carbon emissions, and sustainable aviation fuel, alongside more fuel-efficient aircraft designs, remains the industry's primary near-term response, though it is still far from displacing conventional jet fuel at the scale needed to meaningfully shift the sector's overall emissions.
