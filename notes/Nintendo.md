## In one sentence

Nintendo has outlasted every rival console maker across five decades by refusing to chase raw hardware power, betting instead on cheaper, weirder hardware (a screen you fold shut, two screens, motion control, a screen you can also detach) that only its own games could fully use.

## What it does

Truth be told, Nintendo is older than the light bulb's mass adoption in Japan: it started in 1889 selling handmade hanafuda playing cards in Kyoto, and only entered electronic toys in the 1960s and video games properly in the late 1970s. In the fiscal year to March 2025 it reported revenue of about ¥1.16 trillion (roughly \$7.8 billion), most of it from the Nintendo Switch, which by 2025 had sold over 152 million units since its 2017 launch, close to the lifetime sales of the PlayStation 4.

## Why it keeps surviving

As opposed to Sony and Microsoft, which chase the most powerful chips they can fit in a box, Nintendo has consistently shipped consoles with weaker specifications than its rivals and made up the gap with a different idea entirely. The Wii used a motion controller instead of raw graphics power to sell 101 million units. The Switch is a handheld and a home console in one, an idea Nintendo had already tried with the less successful Wii U, refined until it worked.

In addition, Nintendo owns its own characters (Mario, Zelda, Pokemon through The Pokemon Company, Kirby) and writes most of its own flagship games in-house, so a new console launches with games no rival can copy on day one. This is where the business really makes its margin: hardware often sells close to cost, while first-party software and accessories carry the profit.

## Products and emerging bets

Beyond consoles, Nintendo has pushed into mobile (Pokemon GO through Niantic, Mario Kart Tour), theme parks (Super Nintendo World, opened in Osaka in 2021 and Orlando in 2025), and film, whereby The Super Mario Bros. Movie grossed over \$1.3 billion worldwide in 2023, becoming one of the highest-grossing video game adaptations ever made.

## What surprised me

In our case, the pattern that struck me most is that Nintendo's biggest hits (Wii, Switch) came right after its biggest stumbles (GameCube's weak third-party support, Wii U's confusing pitch). Firstly it under-performs, then it changes the entire premise of the hardware rather than iterating on the failed one, which is a harder discipline to keep than it sounds.
