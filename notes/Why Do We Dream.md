## In one sentence

Nobody has fully proven why we dream, but the leading evidence points to the brain sorting and strengthening memories during REM sleep, a phase where the brain is nearly as active as when awake, while the body stays paralysed so it does not act out whatever the brain is replaying.

## Explain it

Truth be told, dream science is one of the few areas where "why" still does not have one settled answer, only several competing, partly supported theories. What is well established is the mechanism: sleep cycles through stages, and REM sleep, short for rapid eye movement, is the stage where the most vivid dreaming happens, and where the brain shows electrical activity patterns close to being awake, even though the person is deeply asleep.

The leading theory, called memory consolidation, holds that dreaming reflects the brain reactivating and reorganising the day's experiences, strengthening some connections and weakening others, essentially filing memories into longer-term storage. Studies using brain imaging, including research from Matthew Walker, a sleep scientist at UC Berkeley, have found that people who go through REM sleep after learning a task perform measurably better recalling it later than people deprived of REM sleep, which supports dreaming being a byproduct of, or a mechanism for, this filing process.

A separate but related detail explains why dreams do not turn into physical action: during REM sleep the brain releases chemicals that cause muscle atonia, meaning near-total paralysis of the body's voluntary muscles, so a person running from something in a dream stays still in bed. A rare disorder called REM sleep behaviour disorder disables this paralysis, and people with it have been documented physically acting out violent or frightening dreams, which is itself indirect evidence that the paralysis exists specifically to stop that from happening normally.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a student who studies a new skill and then gets a full night of REM-rich sleep typically retains and performs the skill better the next day than a student who studies the same material but is woken up specifically during REM periods, a manipulation used in sleep lab studies.
- Non-example: daydreaming while awake, though it shares the word "dream", is a completely different process, deliberate and semi-controlled imagination during wakefulness, not the largely involuntary imagery generated by the brain during REM sleep.

## Why it matters

So where does this go wrong or matter practically? People deprived of REM sleep for extended periods, through sleep disorders or heavy alcohol or certain medication use, which both suppress REM, show measurable memory and mood problems, suggesting dreaming or at least the REM stage it happens in is not a decorative side effect but doing real cognitive work.

In addition, nightmares, unusually distressing dreams, are studied separately as often reflecting unprocessed stress or trauma, whereby the same memory-sorting process that normally files a day's events smoothly seems to get stuck replaying a specific distressing one repeatedly, which is part of why nightmare frequency is used clinically as one marker for conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder.

In our case, the detail that changed how I think about sleep is that REM deprivation, not just total sleep deprivation, has its own specific cost, which means a full eight hours of light, broken sleep is not the same as eight hours that actually includes enough REM cycles to do this filing work properly.
