## In one sentence

A personality quiz app on Facebook harvested data from 87 million users, most of whom never installed the app themselves but were simply friends with someone who did, and Cambridge Analytica used that data to build psychological profiles for targeted political advertising, a scandal that broke publicly in 2018 and cost Facebook a \$5 billion fine, the largest privacy penalty the US Federal Trade Commission had issued at that point.

## Situation

In 2014, researcher Aleksandr Kogan built a Facebook app called "thisisyourdigitallife", framed as an academic personality quiz, that collected not only data from the roughly 270,000 users who installed it but also, through Facebook's permissions system at the time, data from those users' friends, who had never agreed to anything themselves. This friend-of-friend access expanded the total data pool to an estimated 87 million people. Kogan sold this data to Cambridge Analytica, a political consulting firm, which used it to build psychological profiles intended to help target political advertising, including work for Donald Trump's 2016 US presidential campaign and the Brexit "Leave" campaign in the UK. The story broke publicly in March 2018 through reporting by The Guardian and The New York Times, based on an account from a Cambridge Analytica whistleblower, Christopher Wylie.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Facebook's own platform design at the time allowed an app installed by one person to pull data about that person's entire friend network by default, a setting most users never actively chose or even knew existed, so the "consent" Kogan's app technically obtained from a small number of quiz-takers extended, through the platform's own architecture, to tens of millions of people who gave no consent of any kind.

## What happened

Firstly, Facebook had actually known about Kogan's data harvesting since 2015, when it demanded he and Cambridge Analytica delete the improperly obtained data, but the company did not notify affected users at the time and took the deletion claim at face value without independently verifying it, a gap that became central to later regulatory criticism.

Secondly, once Wylie's account became public in 2018, Facebook's share price dropped sharply, congressional and parliamentary hearings followed in both the US and UK, and CEO Mark Zuckerberg testified before US Congress over two days in April 2018, a level of public scrutiny the company had not faced before.

Thirdly, the US Federal Trade Commission concluded a lengthy investigation in 2019 with a \$5 billion fine against Facebook, alongside new requirements for how it must document and oversee third-party data access going forward, at the time the largest privacy-related penalty ever imposed on a technology company.

Lastly, Cambridge Analytica itself shut down in May 2018, unable to survive the reputational and legal fallout, even though the company always maintained it had not used the data in the way critics alleged.

## Lessons

To begin with, a platform's default settings carry more real consequence than any individual user's specific choices, since the friend-data permission here was opt-out by design, meaning most affected people had implicitly agreed to something they never actively considered.

In addition, knowing about a data misuse privately and quietly demanding deletion, as Facebook did in 2015, is a fundamentally different standard of responsibility than disclosing it to the people actually affected, and regulators eventually treated the gap between those two responses as itself part of the offence.
