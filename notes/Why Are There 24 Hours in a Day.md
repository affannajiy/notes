## In one sentence

Twenty-four hours in a day traces back to ancient Egyptian sundials, which split daylight into twelve equal parts specifically because Egyptian mathematics favoured counting in base 12, and the nighttime hours were later divided into a matching twelve as well, giving 24 total once daytime and nighttime finally got merged into one single, continuous counting system.

## Explain it

Truth be told, ancient Egyptian timekeepers divided daylight hours using sundials into twelve equal segments, a division likely influenced by Egyptian counting practices that favoured base 12, itself possibly linked to counting finger joints, three joints on each of four fingers per hand excluding the thumb, giving twelve countable segments per hand rather than the five whole fingers a base-10 system would naturally suggest. Nighttime hours were tracked separately using a different method, observing the rising of specific stars, and were likewise divided into twelve segments, giving ancient Egypt two separate sets of twelve hours, daytime and nighttime, that varied noticeably in actual length depending on the season, since daylight itself naturally lasts longer in summer and shorter in winter.

This in turn is why the modern system of 24 equal-length hours, fixed consistently regardless of season, only really solidified centuries later, ancient Greek astronomers, Hipparchus among them, proposed dividing the full day into 24 hours of genuinely equal length specifically for more precise and consistent astronomical calculation, though this fixed equal-hour system did not become the standard for ordinary daily life until mechanical clocks became widespread many centuries afterward, finally making equal-length hour tracking practical for everyone rather than only for astronomers.

## Example / non-example

- Example: an ancient Egyptian farmer's working "hour" during a long summer day was genuinely longer in actual duration than a winter "hour," since both simply represented one twelfth of that particular day's daylight regardless of the season's total daylight length, a fundamentally different concept from the fixed, equal-length hour used today.
- Non-example: certain traditional Japanese timekeeping systems used before Western mechanical clocks became standard similarly divided day and night into unequal, seasonally variable segments, showing that Egypt's variable-hour approach was not a unique historical quirk but a genuinely common early solution independently arrived at across different ancient cultures.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond an interesting bit of ancient astronomy history? Firstly, this history directly connects to the base-60 minute and second system covered in this vault's own [[Why Are There 60 Seconds in a Minute]] note, both systems inherited from different ancient civilizations, Egyptian base-12 daylight division and Babylonian base-60 subdivision, later combined together into the single unified 24-hour, 60-minute clock system still used worldwide today.

In addition, the shift from variable, seasonally shifting hours to fixed, equal-length hours reflects a genuinely significant historical transition, from time defined by direct, lived observation of the sun and stars toward time defined by mechanical, abstract measurement, a shift with real consequences for how industrial-era societies eventually organised standardised working hours and schedules.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that the 24-hour day is really the product of two entirely separate ancient counting traditions layered awkwardly on top of each other over many centuries, rather than any single deliberate, unified design choice made all at once.
