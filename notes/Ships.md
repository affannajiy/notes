## In one sentence

Cargo ships are the least visible but most load-bearing part of global trade, moving roughly 80 percent of the world's traded goods by volume, and the industry's defining economics come down to one simple fact, moving freight by sea is dramatically cheaper per tonne than any land or air alternative, which is why it dominates global trade despite being by far the slowest option.

## What it is

A cargo ship is a vessel built specifically to carry goods rather than passengers, ranging from small coastal freighters to some of the largest moving structures humans build, container ships and bulk carriers capable of holding tens of thousands of tonnes of cargo across a single voyage.

## Types

Container ships carry standardised steel shipping containers, a format, covered in this vault's broader coverage of global trade infrastructure, that revolutionised cargo handling by making loading and unloading dramatically faster and more standardised across ports worldwide. Bulk carriers move unpackaged commodities, grain, coal, iron ore, directly in open holds rather than in containers. Tankers carry liquid cargo, crude oil and refined fuel chief among them, and their design, hull structure and safety requirements differ substantially from dry cargo vessels given the specific risks liquid bulk transport carries.

## Terms

A few terms recur across shipping discussion worth defining plainly. "Flag of convenience" describes the practice of registering a ship in a country other than the owner's own, often chosen for lower registration costs, lighter regulation or tax advantages, a widespread and legal practice that nonetheless draws periodic criticism over labour and safety oversight. "Panamax" and "Suezmax," ship size categories, describe the maximum dimensions a vessel can have while still passing through the Panama and Suez canals respectively, a constraint that has directly shaped ship design for decades, since exceeding those dimensions forces a vessel onto a longer, more expensive alternate route.

## History in moments

The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the Panama Canal in 1914 each dramatically shortened key global shipping routes, avoiding the need to sail entirely around Africa or South America, and both canals remain strategically vital enough that a blockage in either can disrupt global trade within days, as the 2021 Ever Given grounding in the Suez Canal demonstrated vividly. The standardisation of the shipping container from the mid-1950s onward, led by American trucking entrepreneur Malcom McLean, is widely credited as the single biggest efficiency leap in shipping history, cutting loading times from days to hours and enabling the scale of global trade seen today.

## Malaysian connection

The Port Klang and Port of Tanjung Pelepas rank among Southeast Asia's busiest container ports, and the Strait of Malacca, running along Malaysia's western coast, is one of the world's most critical shipping chokepoints, carrying a substantial share of global maritime trade between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific.

## What's happening now

The shipping industry faces mounting pressure to decarbonise, since large cargo vessels currently run almost entirely on heavy fuel oil, and alternatives, ammonia and methanol-powered engines among them, remain in early adoption, meaning the sector's emissions reduction, unlike aviation's more visible pressure, has moved comparatively slowly given how large a share of global trade the industry still carries.
