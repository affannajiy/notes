## My take in one line

University is genuinely about learning for a smaller share of what it delivers than most people admit, since a large part of its actual value comes from the credential and the network it hands out regardless of how much of the syllabus was retained, which is why economists can measure a real "sheepskin effect", a pay jump tied to holding the diploma itself, separate from any measurable skill gained.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I have crammed for exams on material I could not explain a week later and still passed with a mark identical, on paper, to genuine understanding, which made me suspicious the credential and the learning are two different products bundled together.

## The case for

Firstly, universities do teach genuinely transferable skills beyond specific facts, critical thinking, structured writing, learning a new field's vocabulary quickly, which are hard to measure directly but show up in how graduates perform on unrelated later tasks, a real form of learning even when course content fades. In addition, fields like medicine and engineering require deep, cumulative technical knowledge that cannot be faked where it matters, a poorly trained doctor causes real harm, so for these fields the learning itself is close to the entire point, credential aside.

Furthermore, exposure to ideas and people outside a student's existing background, a stated goal of most universities, does happen and does change how graduates think, even when hard to isolate from the credential's other effects.

## The case against

On the other hand, labour economists have documented the "sheepskin effect" extensively, whereby wages jump specifically at the point a degree is awarded, not gradually with each year of study, which would not happen if employers were purely paying for skills accumulated smoothly rather than for the credential as a discrete signal. Bryan Caplan's 2018 book The Case Against Education argues most of what a degree signals is conscientiousness, conformity and baseline intelligence, not subject matter, citing surveys showing graduates forget most course content within a few years yet the wage premium persists.

Moreover, networking and access, the people met, the alumni network joined, the internships available through career offices, function as a value stream disconnected from whether any lecture was retained, and this stream is arguably decisive for entire industries where "who you know" opens doors no exam score does.

## Where I land

In my opinion, university genuinely teaches real things but sells a bundle where the credential and network do at least as much of the paying-off as the material, and pretending it is purely about learning ignores why employers so consistently reward the piece of paper over demonstrated skill. In our case, the modules I actually remember years later are a small fraction of everything I sat exams for, yet the degree will keep opening doors regardless of which modules I can still recall.

(To which a lecturer once said, only half joking, "you are not here to remember this, you are here to prove you can learn something under pressure," which is probably the most honest description of university I have heard from someone actually paid to teach it.)

## What would change my mind

Evidence that the sheepskin wage effect shrinks to near zero once controlling carefully for actual measured skill and knowledge retained, rather than for the credential itself, would push me toward believing learning, not signalling, is doing most of the real economic work.
