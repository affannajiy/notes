## In one sentence

Cutting an onion ruptures its cells and triggers a chemical chain reaction that produces a volatile gas called syn-propanethial-S-oxide, which drifts up and reacts with the water in your eyes to form a mild sulfuric acid, and it is that irritation, not the onion's smell itself, that makes eyes water.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "onions release a gas that makes you cry" undersells how deliberate a defence mechanism this actually is. Onions store two separate ingredients in different parts of their cells specifically so they do not react with each other until damage forces them together, sulfur compounds called amino acid sulfoxides in one compartment and an enzyme called alliinase in another. An intact onion cell keeps these apart, but cutting, chopping or even biting an onion ruptures that cellular separation, letting the enzyme and the sulfur compounds mix and react for the first time.

That reaction produces several new sulfur-containing compounds, and the specific one responsible for eye irritation, syn-propanethial-S-oxide, is volatile enough to evaporate into the air around the cutting board and drift upward toward a cutting person's face. Once that gas reaches the thin layer of moisture covering the eye, it reacts with the water there to form a very mild sulfuric acid, and it is that acid, small quantities but still genuinely irritating, that triggers the eye's reflex tear response, flushing the surface to dilute and wash away the irritant.

## Example / non-example

- Example: cooked onions, once heated past a certain point, no longer produce this eye-irritating compound, since heat breaks down the enzyme alliinase before it can keep driving the reaction, which is why sautéing onions never makes anyone cry the way cutting a raw one does.
- Non-example: garlic, a close relative of onion in the same Allium family, contains related sulfur compounds but produces a different, less volatile set of reaction products when cut, which is why garlic has a strong smell but rarely causes the same eye-watering response onions do.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how people cook in practice? Firstly, this is exactly why chilling an onion before cutting is a genuinely useful trick, not folklore, since cold temperature slows the chemical reaction rate and reduces how much of the irritating gas evaporates into the air before it degrades, meaning less of it ever reaches the eyes.

In addition, using a sharp knife rather than a dull one reduces cell rupture overall, since a clean cut damages fewer cells per slice than a dull blade dragging and crushing its way through the onion, which is why professional kitchens treat blade sharpness as a genuine comfort factor during onion prep, not just a matter of speed.

In our case, the detail that reframes the whole thing for me is that this entire response evolved as the onion plant's own defence against being eaten, a chemical deterrent aimed at animals, and it works on human eyes essentially as an accidental side effect of anatomy, not because the onion is doing anything specifically aimed at people cutting it in a kitchen.
