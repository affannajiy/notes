## In one sentence

Airplane windows have rounded corners because sharp corners on early pressurised jet aircraft turned out to be lethally dangerous, a hard lesson learned from a series of catastrophic mid-air structural failures in the 1950s that directly traced back to stress concentrating unevenly around a window's sharp square corners.

## Explain it

Truth be told, when an aircraft climbs to cruising altitude, the cabin is pressurised well above the thin outside air pressure, creating enormous, constantly repeated structural stress across the fuselage's entire surface with every single flight cycle. A sharp corner on a square or rectangular opening concentrates that stress far more intensely at that single point than a smoothly curved edge ever would, since stress travelling through a material has nowhere gradual to distribute itself around an abrupt right angle, making a tiny crack at that exact point far more likely to start forming and then spread catastrophically.

This in turn is exactly the failure mechanism identified after two de Havilland Comet aircraft, the world's first commercial jet airliner, broke apart mid-flight in separate 1954 incidents, extensive investigation eventually tracing both disasters directly to metal fatigue cracks starting at the sharp corners of the plane's square windows. In addition, this same stress-concentration principle applies broadly across engineering generally, not only aircraft windows, which is why rounded corners and edges appear throughout other high-stress structural designs, from ship hulls to spacecraft, wherever repeated pressure or load cycling makes sharp corners a genuine long-term structural liability rather than merely a cosmetic design preference.

## Example / non-example

- Example: modern commercial aircraft windows are universally oval or rounded-rectangle shaped today, a design standard adopted industry-wide directly in response to the Comet disasters, distributing pressurisation stress evenly around the window's curved edge rather than concentrating it at a single sharp point.
- Non-example: a car window's sharp corners cause no comparable danger, since a car's cabin experiences nowhere near the same pressure differential or altitude-driven stress cycling an aircraft fuselage endures at cruising altitude, illustrating that this specific engineering lesson applies specifically to pressurised, high-altitude structures rather than windows generally.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond aircraft window shape trivia? Firstly, the Comet disasters and their investigation are widely taught in engineering education specifically as a foundational case study in metal fatigue, a term describing how repeated stress cycling can crack a material well before it would ever fail under a single one-off load of the same magnitude.

In addition, this history directly shaped modern aviation safety regulation, requiring rigorous structural fatigue testing for new aircraft designs specifically to catch this exact kind of stress-concentration failure long before a plane ever carries paying passengers, a regulatory legacy tracing straight back to a specific, identifiable design flaw.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a design choice as small and easily overlooked as a window's corner shape was once responsible for genuine loss of life, a reminder that engineering safety lessons are frequently learned only after a real disaster forces the underlying physics to be taken seriously.
