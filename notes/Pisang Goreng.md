## In one sentence

Pisang goreng is banana dipped in a thin batter and deep-fried until the outside turns crisp and golden while the inside stays soft and warm, and the specific banana variety used, usually pisang raja or pisang awak, matters more to the result than most people realise, since a banana too ripe collapses in the oil while one too firm never softens properly inside the batter.

## The story

Truth be told, pisang goreng is one of the simplest dishes in this vault's growing food collection, and its simplicity is exactly why it spread so widely across Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore without any single country's claim to it becoming a serious dispute the way [[Rendang]] or [[Satay]]'s origins have. Fried banana snacks of one kind or another appear across much of Southeast Asia, and the Malay Peninsula's specific batter-dipped, deep-fried version likely developed alongside other fried street snacks sold from the same stalls, gorengan (assorted fried snacks) being a whole category pisang goreng sits inside rather than a dish that stands entirely alone.

The dish's appeal rests partly on how cheap and accessible it has always been, sold from roadside stalls and night markets for a fraction of the price of almost anything else in this vault's food notes, which is part of why it remains a genuinely everyday snack rather than something reserved for special occasions the way [[Kuih]] sometimes is.

## Why it is the way it is

Under the hood, the batter is typically a simple mix of flour, a little rice flour for extra crispness, sugar, and sometimes turmeric for colour, thinned with water into a coating just heavy enough to crisp in hot oil without smothering the banana's own flavour underneath. Firstly, the banana variety choice genuinely changes the result, since pisang raja and pisang awak hold their shape and starchiness well under heat, staying pleasantly firm rather than turning to mush, unlike a fully ripe Cavendish banana, the common supermarket variety, which is often too soft and sweet for frying.

Secondly, oil temperature control matters more here than in most fried dishes, since banana releases moisture as it cooks and oil that is not hot enough produces a soggy, oil-heavy batter rather than the crisp shell a good pisang goreng needs, which is why stall vendors keep the oil at a visibly aggressive bubble rather than a gentle simmer.

## The variants

| Name | What changes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Pisang goreng classic | Plain banana in crisp batter |
| Pisang goreng cheese | Grated or shredded cheese added on top after frying |
| Pisang goreng with ice cream | Served warm alongside cold ice cream, a common dessert pairing |
| Kolak pisang (Indonesia) | Banana simmered in coconut milk and palm sugar instead of fried |

## Where I met it

In our case, pisang goreng is the snack I associate with rain, since Malaysian rainstorms and the smell of banana frying from a nearby stall seem to arrive together often enough that I now genuinely crave one the moment the sky darkens.
