## In one sentence

Chilli peppers do not actually raise the temperature of anything in a person's mouth, the compound capsaicin they contain simply binds to the exact same nerve receptor that normally detects genuine physical heat, which is why the brain, receiving an identical warning signal either way, has no real way to tell the difference between a hot chilli and an actual burn.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the sensation of "hot" from spicy food and the sensation of hot from a literal flame both travel through the same biological pathway, a heat-sensing receptor on nerve cells called TRPV1, normally triggered by temperatures above roughly 43 degrees Celsius. Capsaicin, the active compound in chilli peppers, happens to bind directly to that same TRPV1 receptor at any temperature, tricking it into firing the exact same "this is hot" signal it would normally only send in response to genuine heat.

This in turn is why capsaicin's effect is measured on the Scoville scale, quantifying roughly how much a chilli's capsaicin concentration needs to be diluted before human tasters can no longer detect any heat sensation at all, a scale ranging from mild jalapeños in the low thousands up to the world's hottest cultivated peppers exceeding two million Scoville units. In addition, capsaicin also triggers a genuine physiological stress response, sweating, a racing heart, sometimes tears, essentially the same reaction the body produces facing actual danger, since as far as the nervous system can tell, it is currently facing something dangerously hot.

## Example / non-example

- Example: eating a very hot curry can genuinely make someone sweat and their heart rate rise measurably, a real physiological stress response triggered purely by a chemical signal rather than any actual increase in body or mouth temperature.
- Non-example: menthol, found in mint and used in some cooling products, works through the opposite mechanism, binding to the cold-sensing TRPM8 receptor even at a normal room temperature, producing a convincing cooling sensation without the surface actually getting any colder at all.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond explaining a curry's kick? Firstly, this exact mechanism explains why drinking water rarely helps much after eating something too spicy, capsaicin is fat-soluble rather than water-soluble, meaning water simply spreads the compound around the mouth rather than washing it away, while milk or yoghurt genuinely helps because their fat content can actually dissolve and clear the capsaicin.

In addition, capsaicin's ability to activate pain receptors specifically has led to legitimate medical uses, topical creams containing it are used to treat certain kinds of chronic nerve pain, deliberately overstimulating the same pain pathway until it temporarily becomes less responsive overall, a genuinely counterintuitive treatment that only makes sense once the underlying receptor mechanism is understood.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that "spicy hot" and "genuinely hot" are, from the nervous system's own perspective, indistinguishable signals, which is exactly why the same word "hot" gets used for both in nearly every language, a linguistic pattern that turns out to track real underlying biology rather than being pure coincidence.
