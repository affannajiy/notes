## In one line

The pandemic pulled two years of Netflix growth into six months, the crash in 2022 was the bill, and the fix was two decisions it had avoided for years: ads and charging password sharers.

## Situation

Netflix ended 2019 with 167 million subscribers. Lockdown added about 26 million in the first half of 2020 alone, more than a normal year. Then in April 2022 it reported its first subscriber loss in over a decade, 200,000, followed by 970,000 more, and the share price fell from about \$700 to about \$166, a drop of roughly 75%.

Four years later, in January 2026, it reported 2025 revenue of \$45.2 billion, an operating margin of 29.5% and 325 million subscribers. So the question at hand is how a company that looked finished in mid-2022 ended up bigger than at the peak of lockdown.

## Core problem

About 100 million households were watching on someone else's password. Netflix had spotted this internally in 2019, then the lockdown surge arrived and the effort was quietly dropped, because why annoy people when the numbers are going up on their own. Meanwhile Disney+, HBO Max, Peacock and Paramount+ all launched between 2019 and 2021. In Layman's terms, Netflix was giving away roughly a third of its audience for free while its competitors charged for theirs.

## What fixed it

Firstly, ads. In November 2022 Netflix launched an ad plan at \$6.99, which Reed Hastings had said for years it would never do. Ad revenue reached \$1.5 billion in 2025.

Secondly, the password crackdown from May 2023, whereby an account is tied to one household and anyone outside it pays extra. Truth be told, I expected this to blow up in their faces. Instead Netflix added 57 million subscribers between June 2022 and June 2024.

Thirdly, it changed what it measures. From 2025 it stopped reporting subscriber numbers and told Wall Street to watch revenue and margin, which is the sign of a business that is no longer a land grab.

## What happened next

In December 2025 Netflix agreed to buy Warner Bros and HBO for about \$72 billion. Paramount Skydance came back with a hostile all-cash bid, and on 26 February 2026 Netflix walked away and collected a \$2.8 billion break fee. The pandemic winner could afford to lose an \$80 billion auction and treat the consolation as a rounding error.

## Lessons

A windfall is not growth. Treating the lockdown subscribers as a trend is what made 2022 look like a collapse instead of a correction. In our case as students, an A on an easy paper is the same thing.

In addition, the decision you keep postponing is usually the one that fixes it. Netflix sat on the password problem for three years because good numbers made it easy not to, and the crackdown ended up as the biggest driver of the recovery.
