## In one sentence

Popcorn pops because each kernel holds a small amount of water sealed inside a hard, nearly waterproof outer shell, and when heat turns that trapped water into pressurised steam faster than the shell can vent it, the shell eventually ruptures explosively, turning the kernel's dense starch centre inside out into the familiar fluffy shape.

## Explain it

Truth be told, popcorn is genuinely a different variety of corn from the sweet corn eaten off the cob, not just a preparation method, called Zea mays everta, bred specifically to have a shell hard and waterproof enough to hold pressure the way this whole process requires. Every kernel contains roughly 14 percent water by weight sealed inside that hard outer hull, called the pericarp, surrounding a dense, starchy interior.

As a kernel heats, that internal water begins converting to steam, and because the pericarp is nearly impermeable, the steam has nowhere to escape and pressure builds steadily inside the sealed kernel, reaching roughly 135 pounds per square inch, about nine times normal atmospheric pressure, before the shell finally fails at somewhere around 180 degrees Celsius. At that rupture point, the pressure releases so quickly that the soft, gelatinised starch inside expands almost instantly, turning the interior inside out into a foam-like structure that then cools and hardens into the familiar crunchy popped shape within a fraction of a second.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a kernel with an intact, undamaged pericarp reliably builds enough internal pressure to pop, which is why popcorn packaging and storage specifically try to avoid cracking the shells before cooking.
- Non-example: "old maids," the kernels that fail to pop in a batch, have usually lost too much of their internal moisture through a cracked shell or improper storage, meaning they cannot build sufficient steam pressure before the heat simply dries them out instead, a failure mode entirely explained by moisture loss rather than randomness.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond snack trivia? Firstly, this is exactly why popcorn kernels are stored in sealed, moisture-controlled packaging rather than left exposed to open air, since losing even a modest fraction of that internal 14 percent moisture content is often enough to push a kernel below the threshold needed to pop reliably.

In addition, the two main popped shapes, "snowflake," an irregular, wing-like shape used in most packaged and cinema popcorn, and "mushroom," a rounder, denser shape preferred for caramel corn because it holds coating better, come down to small genetic and moisture differences between corn varieties, not the cooking method, which is why manufacturers deliberately choose different popcorn varieties depending on the intended product.

In our case, the detail that stands out most is how precisely this is a pressure vessel failure, the same underlying physics behind why a sealed can heated too far can rupture, just engineered by the plant itself into something edible and, as it turns out, genuinely enjoyable.
