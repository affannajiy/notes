## In one sentence

Physical newspaper circulation and advertising revenue collapsed over roughly two decades as classified ads and general advertising moved online, mostly to Google and Facebook rather than to newspapers' own websites, breaking a business model that had funded journalism for over a century.

## Situation

For most of the 20th century, newspapers ran on a fairly stable model: subscription and newsstand sales covered part of the cost, and advertising, particularly classified ads for jobs, cars, real estate and personal notices, covered the rest, often the larger share. That classified advertising business alone was worth tens of billions of dollars annually in the United States at its peak through the 1990s. The core problem at hand is that this advertising, once a newspaper's most reliable and highest-margin revenue source, had no inherent loyalty to print, it existed to reach an audience, and once a cheaper, more targeted way to reach that audience appeared online, advertisers had every reason to move, and little reason to stay.

## Core problem

The one tension newspapers faced was that the internet did not merely compete with them for readers, it specifically targeted the exact revenue stream, classified and display advertising, that had subsidised journalism's actual cost of production, while online news reading itself trained readers to expect news for free, undermining the subscription side of the model at the same time from a different direction.

## What happened

Firstly, dedicated classified websites, Craigslist launched in 1995 and eBay in the same year among the earliest, offered free or near-free listings that made a newspaper's paid classified section look expensive and inconvenient by comparison, and that single revenue category collapsed fastest and hardest of all.

Secondly, Google and Facebook grew into the two dominant destinations for online advertising generally, since both could target ads with far more precision, by search intent or demographic and behavioural data, than a newspaper's broad readership ever could, capturing the general advertising revenue that briefly seemed like it might migrate to newspapers' own websites instead.

Thirdly, most newspapers initially gave their content away free online, a decision made before anyone fully understood the coming advertising collapse, training an entire generation of readers to expect news without payment, a habit that later paywalls have only partially reversed.

Lastly, newsroom staffing across the US and many other countries fell sharply through the 2000s and 2010s as revenue shrank, and numerous local papers closed entirely, creating what researchers now call "news deserts," communities left with little to no dedicated local news coverage at all.

## Lessons

To begin with, a business built on being the best available channel to reach an audience is only as durable as that channel advantage, and newspapers held classified and display advertising for a century mainly because no cheaper, more precise alternative existed yet, not because of any lasting structural advantage of print itself.

In addition, giving a product away for free while an industry is still deciding on a long-term model is a decision that is far easier to make than to later undo, since audience expectations, once set, resist reversal even after the underlying economics that justified the free version have completely changed.
