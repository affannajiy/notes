## In one sentence

MSG got its bad name from a single 1968 letter to a medical journal describing symptoms after eating at Chinese restaurants, decades of research since then have failed to reproduce those symptoms in controlled testing, and the compound itself is just the sodium salt of glutamate, an amino acid that is already in tomatoes, parmesan and your own body.

## Explain it

MSG, short for monosodium glutamate, is a white crystalline seasoning made by pairing glutamate, an amino acid, meaning one of the building blocks that make up protein, with sodium. Truth be told, glutamate itself is not exotic. It occurs naturally in tomatoes, parmesan cheese, soy sauce, mushrooms and breast milk, and the human body produces and uses glutamate constantly as a neurotransmitter, a chemical that carries signals between nerve cells.

The scare began in 1968, when a doctor wrote a letter to the New England Journal of Medicine describing numbness, weakness and palpitations after eating at Chinese restaurants, coining the term "Chinese restaurant syndrome". The letter was an anecdote, not a study, yet it spread fast enough that MSG became the default explanation for any discomfort after Chinese food specifically, as opposed to any other cuisine that also uses it. Since then, dozens of double-blind trials, whereby neither the researcher nor the subject knows who received MSG versus a placebo, have tried to reproduce the reported symptoms and mostly failed to find a consistent effect at normal food doses.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a bowl of ramen seasoned with MSG and a bowl of ramen made rich with a long-simmered pork bone broth taste similarly savoury, because both are delivering glutamate to the same taste receptors, the broth simply takes eight hours to do what a pinch of MSG does in one second.
- Non-example: a small minority of people report genuine sensitivity to large doses of MSG taken without food, several grams on an empty stomach, in some clinical trials, but this is a different and much rarer claim than the general "MSG is dangerous" belief, and normal cooking amounts, a gram or two per dish, sit well below those test doses.

## Why it matters

So where does the bias actually come from, if not the chemistry? Food historians and the doctor's own later writing suggest the letter tapped into anti-Chinese sentiment in 1960s America, since near-identical amounts of glutamate in Italian parmesan or Japanese dashi never got the same suspicion or the same nickname. The US Food and Drug Administration classifies MSG as generally recognised as safe, the same category as salt and sugar, and Japan, where MSG was first isolated from seaweed broth in 1908 by chemist Kikunae Ikeda, has used it in cooking for over a century without the same stigma.

In our case, the practical lesson is to separate the taste from the label. Umami, the fifth basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter, is what glutamate provides, and food gets less flavourful, not safer, when a cook avoids MSG purely on reputation instead of on any actual reaction they have felt.
