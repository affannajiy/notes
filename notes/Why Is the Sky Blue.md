## In one sentence

The sky looks blue because sunlight, which contains every visible colour mixed together, scatters off the tiny molecules in the atmosphere far more strongly at the blue end of the spectrum than the red end, a wavelength-dependent effect called Rayleigh scattering that spreads blue light across the whole sky while letting the rest continue mostly straight through.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the honest short answer, "because blue light scatters more," sounds almost too simple until you see why wavelength matters so much here. Sunlight looks white but is really a mix of every colour, and each colour corresponds to a specific wavelength of light, red having a longer wavelength and blue a shorter one. When that light hits the atmosphere, it collides with nitrogen and oxygen molecules far smaller than any of those wavelengths, and physicist Lord Rayleigh worked out in the 1870s that this kind of scattering off very small particles is strongly wavelength-dependent, scaling roughly with the inverse fourth power of wavelength.

That relationship means blue light, with its shorter wavelength, scatters roughly ten times more strongly than red light of a longer wavelength. This in turn means that as sunlight passes through the atmosphere, its blue component gets knocked around and redirected in every direction far more than the red and yellow components, which is why looking at any patch of clear sky, not just directly at the sun, still shows blue, the light reaching your eye there arrived by scattering rather than travelling straight from the sun.

## Example / non-example

- Example: at sunset, sunlight travels through a much longer stretch of atmosphere to reach an observer, scattering away nearly all the blue light before it arrives, leaving mostly the red and orange wavelengths that scatter less, which is why sunsets shift toward those warmer colours while midday sky, seen through a shorter atmospheric path, stays blue.
- Non-example: on the Moon, which has essentially no atmosphere to scatter light at all, the sky appears black even in full daylight, confirming that atmosphere, not sunlight itself, is what produces a blue sky.

## Why it matters

So why does this particular detail of physics matter beyond satisfying curiosity? Firstly, the same Rayleigh scattering principle explains why the sky is not violet, even though violet light has an even shorter wavelength than blue and scatters even more strongly, the answer being that sunlight itself contains less violet to begin with, and the human eye's colour receptors are more sensitive to blue than violet, so the combined effect nets out as blue rather than violet.

In addition, this scattering principle extends well beyond the sky itself, playing a role in why distant mountains often look bluish-grey, called aerial perspective, a haze effect landscape painters have long used deliberately to suggest depth and distance in a scene.

In our case, the detail that stuck with me most is that the sky is not actually "blue" as some fixed property, it is closer to a live, physics-driven display shifting continuously with the sun's angle and the thickness of atmosphere the light travels through, which is also exactly why sunrise, midday and sunset skies never look quite the same twice.
