## In one sentence

A plane stays up because its wings are shaped and angled to push air downward as they cut through it, and by Newton's third law that downward push on the air creates an equal upward push on the wing, called lift, strong enough to outweigh the plane once it moves fast enough.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the popular explanation involving Bernoulli's principle, whereby air moving faster over the curved top of a wing creates lower pressure than the flatter air underneath, is not wrong, but it is incomplete on its own and often taught in a way that implies the curve alone explains flight. Paper airplanes and flat-bottomed stunt planes fly too, and neither relies on a curved top surface the way that explanation demands.

The fuller picture is that a wing is tilted slightly upward at the front, an angle called the angle of attack, so incoming air gets deflected downward as it passes both over and under the wing. Deflecting a mass of air downward means the wing exerts a downward force on that air, and Newton's third law says every force has an equal and opposite reaction, so the air pushes back upward on the wing with the same force. This reaction force is lift, and the faster the plane moves or the greater the angle of attack, up to a point, the more air gets deflected and the more lift is generated.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a hand held flat out of a moving car window, tilted slightly so the front edge is higher than the back, feels an upward push, the same deflection principle at a tiny scale, which is why pilots-in-training are often told to try this to feel lift directly.
- Non-example: a flat sheet of paper held level in still air generates no lift and simply falls, because there is no forward airspeed to deflect and no angle to deflect it downward with. Speed and angle both have to be present together.

## Why it matters

So where does this go wrong for a plane in trouble? Stalling, a dangerous state pilots train for constantly, happens when the angle of attack gets too steep: air can no longer flow smoothly over the wing and instead separates into turbulent eddies, whereby the deflection breaks down and lift collapses suddenly rather than gradually. This is why pilots point the nose down to recover from a stall rather than pulling up further, since pulling up only increases the angle that caused the problem.

In addition, this is why heavier planes need longer runways and higher takeoff speeds: lift has to grow to match weight before the plane leaves the ground, and the only two variables a pilot controls at that point are speed and angle. Boeing 747s take off around 290 km/h fully loaded, while a small Cessna can lift off under 100 km/h, purely because of how much weight each wing has to overcome.

In our case, the next time I am on a flight and feel the plane tilt its nose up right after takeoff, that tilt is the pilot deliberately increasing the angle of attack for a moment to climb faster, trading a bit of speed for more lift, exactly the trade the physics above describes.
