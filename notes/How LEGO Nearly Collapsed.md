## In one sentence

LEGO spent the late 1990s and early 2000s building theme parks, clothing lines and video games on the belief that the brick alone could not carry the company's growth, and by 2004 it was close enough to bankruptcy that the family that owned it brought in an outsider with no toy experience to save it.

## Situation

The LEGO Group is a Danish toymaker founded by Ole Kirk Christiansen in 1932 and still owned by his family. Through the 1990s, as computer and video games ate into toy sales worldwide, LEGO responded by expanding far outside bricks: it opened LEGOLAND parks in Denmark, the UK, Germany and the US, launched clothing and watch lines, and moved into video games and television.

By 2003 that expansion had not paid off. Revenue fell by roughly 26% that year, and in 2004 the company posted a loss of about DKK 1.4 billion on revenue of about DKK 6.8 billion, meaning it lost around one krone for every five it brought in. Cash reserves ran low enough that the family considered selling outright.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that LEGO's leadership believed the brick had stopped being enough to grow the business, so instead of making the core product better it spent money proving that theory everywhere else. Meanwhile the sets it still sold under the brick name had drifted from their own strength: piece counts and instruction steps had climbed every year, so the toy meant to be simple to build had become hard to build, the opposite of what made it popular.

## What happened

Firstly, the board replaced Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen, the family's own third-generation leader, with Jørgen Vig Knudstorp in 2004, an outsider who had never run a toy company and had spent his prior career at McKinsey. That alone signalled the family accepted the crisis was structural, not a bad year.

Secondly, Knudstorp sold the LEGOLAND parks to Merlin Entertainments in 2005 for about £46 million plus a 30% stake, cash out for a business treated as core just two years earlier.

Thirdly, he audited every part number in the catalogue and cut the total from over 12,900 unique pieces to about 7,000, removing the ones that cost the most to produce relative to how often they were used, without touching the brick design itself.

Lastly, instead of inventing new categories, LEGO leaned harder into the one thing that had already worked without anyone fully crediting it: the 1999 Star Wars licence. Renewing and expanding licensed lines (Star Wars, later Harry Potter) rebuilt sales faster than any new theme could have.

## Lessons

To begin with, a crisis caused by drifting from the core product is fixed by returning to it, not by drifting further. LEGOLAND and the clothing lines were symptoms of the same instinct, growth anywhere except the brick, and cutting them was the fix, not a retreat.

In addition, complexity is a cost even when customers do not complain about it directly. Nobody wrote to LEGO asking for fewer piece types, yet cutting them from 12,900 to 7,000 is what let the company afford to keep making the bricks people actually wanted.

For me, I feel the sharpest lesson is that the family itself had to admit an outsider would see the business more clearly than they could, which is a harder thing for an owner to do than any of the financial cuts that followed.
