## In one sentence

Caps Lock exists as a direct holdover from mechanical typewriters, where it served a genuinely important physical function locking the typewriter's shift mechanism in place so a typist did not need to hold down a stiff shift key continuously while typing an entire word or line in capitals, a function that survived onto computer keyboards decades after the mechanical need behind it had disappeared entirely.

## Explain it

Truth be told, mechanical typewriters worked by physically shifting the entire typebar basket or carriage upward when the shift key was pressed, exposing capital letters instead of lowercase ones, and holding that shift key down continuously for an extended stretch of capitalised text was genuinely tiring on the fingers given how much physical spring resistance older typewriters typically had. A shift lock mechanism, mechanically clicking into a held-down position, solved this directly, letting a typist type an entire capitalised passage without needing to maintain constant physical pressure on the key throughout.

This in turn is exactly why the feature carried straight over onto early computer keyboards even though computers obviously have no actual mechanical shift carriage that genuinely needs locking in place, the key simply toggles a software flag instead, but the muscle memory and expected convenience of a dedicated capitals-lock function had already become deeply familiar to typists trained on decades of prior typewriter use, making its removal a much harder sell than simply keeping a now-vestigial key around. In addition, Caps Lock has become one of computing's most consistently criticised keyboard keys precisely because it occupies prime, easily reachable real estate right next to the important A key, yet gets used deliberately far less often than many far less prominently placed keys, a frequent source of accidental all-caps typing when a user's finger misses its intended target slightly.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a typist accustomed to older manual typewriters genuinely relied on a shift lock mechanism to type headlines or emphasised passages in capitals without fatiguing their fingers holding shift down across many consecutive keystrokes.
- Non-example: modern text formatting tools, bold, italics or a simple keyboard shortcut to convert selected text to uppercase after the fact, have largely made Caps Lock's original convenience function genuinely unnecessary for most everyday typing tasks, which is exactly why several software and hardware designers have proposed remapping or removing the key entirely in more recent keyboard designs.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a mildly annoying keyboard quirk? Firstly, this history is a genuinely useful example of how a physical mechanical necessity can persist as a purely conventional digital feature long after the underlying hardware reason for its existence has disappeared entirely, a pattern echoed by the QWERTY keyboard layout itself, covered separately in this vault's [[Why Do Keyboards Use QWERTY]] note.

In addition, some more recent keyboard and operating system designs have begun offering the option to remap Caps Lock to a more genuinely useful modern function, a shortcut key for switching languages or triggering a search bar being common alternatives, reflecting a slow but real shift away from blindly preserving typewriter-era convention purely out of habit.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that Caps Lock's prominent keyboard position was never really about how often the function gets used, it was inherited directly from typewriter design decisions made for reasons that simply no longer apply to how people actually type today.
