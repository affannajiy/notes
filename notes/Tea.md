## In one sentence

Every major tea type, green, black, oolong and white, actually comes from the exact same plant, Camellia sinensis, and the wildly different flavours and colours between them come almost entirely from one variable applied afterward, how much the picked leaves are allowed to oxidise before processing stops that oxidation permanently.

## What it is

Tea is a beverage made by steeping processed leaves of the Camellia sinensis plant in hot water, distinct from herbal infusions like chamomile or peppermint, which are commonly called "tea" in everyday speech but technically contain no actual tea leaf at all.

## Types

Green tea, made by heating freshly picked leaves quickly to halt oxidation almost immediately, retains a lighter colour and a grassier, more vegetal flavour closest to the plant's natural state. Black tea, made by allowing leaves to fully oxidise before drying, develops the darker colour and stronger, often malty flavour familiar from most Western breakfast tea blends. Oolong tea sits deliberately between the two, partially oxidised for a duration a tea maker controls carefully, producing a genuinely wide flavour range depending on exactly how far that partial oxidation is taken.

## Terms

A few terms recur across tea discussion worth defining plainly. "Oxidation," the chemical reaction between compounds in the tea leaf and oxygen in the air once a leaf is picked and its cell walls damaged through rolling or bruising, is the single process responsible for most differences between tea types, not fermentation, a common but technically inaccurate description. "Terroir," a term borrowed from wine, describes how a tea's specific growing region, soil, altitude and climate meaningfully shapes its final flavour, which is why teas grown in different regions from the same plant variety still taste noticeably distinct.

## History in moments

Tea cultivation in China dates back at least two thousand years, with legend crediting the emperor Shen Nong around 2737 BC, though the earliest solid archaeological evidence points to genuine tea drinking culture developing during the Han dynasty. The Dutch East India Company introduced tea to Europe in the early 17th century, and Britain's later adoption of tea as a national drink was driven partly by its colonial control of tea plantations in India, particularly Assam, established through the 19th century. "Teh tarik," Malaysia's own distinctive pulled milk tea, developed through Indian Muslim immigrant communities in the early 20th century, the pulling technique itself, pouring tea back and forth between two vessels, serving to cool and aerate the drink into its signature frothy texture, a note already covered in this vault's [[Teh Tarik]] entry.

## Malaysian connection

Beyond teh tarik, Malaysia's Cameron Highlands region has grown tea commercially since the British colonial era, its cooler highland climate providing genuinely suitable growing conditions rarely found elsewhere in the country's largely tropical lowlands.

## What's happening now

Specialty and single-origin tea culture has grown significantly worldwide, mirroring the earlier specialty coffee movement, with drinkers increasingly willing to pay a premium for tea traceable to a specific farm and harvest rather than a generic blended product.
