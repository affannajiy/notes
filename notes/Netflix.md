## In one sentence

Netflix moved from a DVD-by-mail rental company into the company that effectively created the modern subscription streaming market, and today makes money almost entirely from monthly subscription fees rather than advertising, a structural choice only recently starting to change.

## What it does

Netflix operates a subscription video streaming service available in most countries, offering a library of licensed films and television alongside a large and growing slate of Netflix-produced original content, accessible on demand across phones, smart TVs, game consoles and computers rather than on any fixed broadcast schedule.

## How it makes money

Historically, Netflix's revenue came almost entirely from monthly subscription fees paid directly by members, a simpler model than most media companies, which typically also rely on advertising. This changed in 2023, when Netflix introduced a cheaper, advertising-supported tier partly in response to slowing subscriber growth and pressure from competing services, meaning advertising now supplements, though does not yet dominate, subscription revenue as a second income stream. Netflix also cracked down on password sharing outside a single household around the same period, converting previously unpaid shared use into either new paid accounts or an extra-member fee, a direct and fairly blunt way of capturing revenue the company had tolerated for years.

## Products

Beyond its core streaming library, Netflix has expanded into original film and television production at a scale that makes it a genuine studio, not just a distributor, commissioning content directly rather than solely licensing it from other studios as it did in its early streaming years. It has also moved into mobile gaming, bundling a modest catalogue of games into existing subscriptions rather than charging separately, though this remains a minor part of the business compared to video. Firstly, this original-content strategy was a defensive necessity as much as ambition, since the major studios that once licensed Netflix their catalogues, as covered in [[The Rise of Streaming|the broader industry pattern]], increasingly pulled that content back once they recognised streaming's importance and launched their own competing services instead.

In addition, Netflix's data on what subscribers actually watch, rather than what they claim to want, has shaped its commissioning decisions in a way traditional studios, reliant on test screenings and focus groups, historically could not match, though this advantage has narrowed as competitors built similar data capabilities of their own.
