## In one sentence

The voltage split between roughly 240-volt and 120-volt countries traces back to a genuine early engineering trade-off, Thomas Edison's original low-voltage systems being safer but far less efficient at delivering power over distance, and different countries simply locked in different early decisions during electrification that later proved extremely costly and disruptive to ever fully reverse.

## Explain it

Truth be told, Thomas Edison's earliest commercial electrical systems in the 1880s ran on relatively low voltage, around 110 volts, chosen partly because lower voltage was genuinely safer for the exposed early wiring and equipment of that era, but this lower voltage also meant more electrical current was needed to deliver the same total power, and higher current causes more energy loss as heat travelling through wires over distance, meaning low-voltage systems required thicker, more expensive cabling and worked best only across relatively short distances from a power source.

This in turn is why George Westinghouse's competing alternating current systems, and later European electrification efforts starting somewhat after America's, favoured higher voltage instead, around 220 to 240 volts, since higher voltage delivers the same total power using less current, meaning thinner, cheaper wiring and genuinely more efficient transmission across longer distances between power stations and homes. The United States had already invested heavily in its lower-voltage infrastructure before this efficiency advantage became fully clear, and by the time higher voltage's benefits were well understood, switching an entire already-electrified country's wiring, appliances and infrastructure over would have been extraordinarily expensive and disruptive.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a traveller bringing a 120-volt American hair dryer to a 240-volt country and plugging it in without a proper voltage converter, not merely a plug shape adapter, risks damaging the appliance or causing an electrical hazard, since the two systems are genuinely incompatible in electrical terms, not simply in socket shape.
- Non-example: many modern laptop and phone chargers are actually built to handle both voltage ranges automatically, a design feature that has made international travel with electronics genuinely much simpler than it was decades ago, even though the underlying household wiring standard itself has never actually converged between countries.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond an inconvenient travel adapter problem? Firstly, this history is a genuinely useful example of "path dependence," a technical concept describing how an early decision, reasonable at the time it was made, can lock a system into a permanently different, and sometimes globally less efficient, standard purely because reversing it later became too costly relative to the benefit.

In addition, some countries that electrified relatively later, much of Africa and parts of Asia among them, were able to skip the low-voltage phase entirely and adopt the more efficient higher-voltage standard from the very start, precisely because they had no existing low-voltage infrastructure investment yet locking them into the older approach.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that voltage standards are not really an engineering choice anymore for most existing countries, they are an inherited historical commitment, similar in kind to the QWERTY keyboard layout covered in this vault's own [[Why Do Keyboards Use QWERTY]] note, where an early practical decision became permanently embedded well beyond its original justification.
