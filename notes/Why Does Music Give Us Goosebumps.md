## In one sentence

Certain moments in music, usually a sudden shift in volume, harmony or a voice breaking through an expectation, trigger the same brain reward system that responds to food or a good surprise, releasing dopamine and setting off a reflex left over from when raised hairs actually helped our ancestors trap heat or look bigger to a predator.

## Explain it

The scientific name for music-induced goosebumps is frisson, meaning a shiver, and researchers estimate roughly half to two thirds of people experience it regularly. Truth be told, the hairs standing up (piloerection, the same reflex behind goosebumps in the cold) is a leftover survival mechanism: in furrier ancestors, raised hairs trapped warm air or made the animal look larger to a threat. Music has nothing to do with temperature or danger, yet it borrows the same nervous system pathway.

A 2011 study using brain imaging, led by researcher Valorie Salimpoor, found that frisson moments coincide with a dopamine release in the brain's reward centres, the same chemical involved in eating, romance and addictive behaviour, and that the release actually peaks in anticipation just before the goosebump moment, not only during it. This is why the exact same swelling chorus can give a listener chills on the fortieth listen just as reliably as the first, since the brain has learned when the payoff is coming and rewards the buildup itself.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a sudden unexpected key change, a solo voice entering after a long orchestral build, or a held note that resolves after tension, are the classic triggers researchers point to, because each one sets up an expectation and then breaks or fulfils it in a way slightly outside what the listener predicted.
- Non-example: background music playing at a steady, familiar volume with no surprising shift rarely produces frisson, because there is no violated or delayed expectation for the brain to reward. Predictable music soothes; it does not usually give chills.

## Why it matters

So why does this differ so much between people? Studies, including one from 2016 in the journal Oxford Economic Papers on personality and frisson, found that people who score high on a personality trait called "openness to experience" report chills far more often than others, which suggests the reflex is partly about how much a listener's brain engages with and predicts the structure of what it is hearing, not just how loud or dramatic a song objectively is.

In addition, this is why the same song rarely gives chills to two different people at the exact same moment, and why a song loses its frisson effect for a listener after enough repetitions dull the element of surprise, even while the same listener may still enjoy the song emotionally. The reward system, it turns out, cares less about the sound itself and more about whether the sound outran what the brain expected to hear next.

In our case, I notice this most reliably during a live performance rather than a recording, since a live vocal run or an unplanned crowd moment carries a genuine unpredictability that a recording, heard the fortieth time, has to work much harder to fake.
