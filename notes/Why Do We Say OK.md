## In one sentence

"OK," now arguably the most widely recognised word on the planet, most likely began in 1839 as a Boston newspaper joke, a deliberately silly misspelled abbreviation of "all correct" written as "oll korrect," and it only survived as a genuine word at all because it happened to catch a lucky break during an American presidential campaign the following year.

## Explain it

Truth be told, American newspapers in the 1830s went through a genuine fad for playful abbreviations built from intentional misspellings, a joke format where "no go" became "K.G." and "all correct" became "O.K.," and most of these abbreviations faded quickly once the fad passed. "OK" specifically survived because of a stroke of political luck, President Martin Van Buren's 1840 re-election campaign nicknamed him "Old Kinderhook" after his New York hometown, and supporters formed "OK Clubs" that campaigned loudly using the initials, permanently cementing the abbreviation in the public's mind well beyond the original newspaper joke.

This in turn is why etymologist Allen Walker Read, who traced this exact origin through careful archival research in the 1960s, is widely credited with settling a long-running debate, since "OK" had previously been claimed to derive from everything from a Native American word to a French phrase to a Haitian port name, none of which held up under his more rigorous documentary evidence. In addition, "OK" spread globally with unusual speed and ease specifically because its two simple letters are pronounceable and recognisable across an extremely wide range of different languages and writing systems, a linguistic accident that helped it become genuinely universal.

## Example / non-example

- Example: "OK" functioning equally well as agreement, acknowledgement or a simple filler word, "OK, let's go," "the food was OK," "OK then," demonstrates the word's genuinely unusual flexibility across completely different grammatical roles within a single language.
- Non-example: many other 1830s Boston newspaper abbreviation jokes from the exact same fad, "K.G." for "no go" among them, never survived at all, having lacked the same lucky political attachment that specifically kept "OK" alive well past its original joke context.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a fun bit of word trivia? Firstly, this origin story is a genuinely useful example of how language change often depends on chance events entirely disconnected from a word's original meaning, a political campaign slogan mattering more to the word's survival than the newspaper joke that actually created it.

In addition, "OK" is frequently cited by linguists as one of the most successfully globally exported English words, appearing as a loanword recognisably across dozens of unrelated languages, a spread that reflects both American cultural influence through the 20th century and the word's own unusually simple, easy-to-adopt phonetic shape.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a word's ubiquity today says nothing reliable about how seriously, or unseriously, it actually began, "OK" started life as a throwaway newspaper pun that most readers at the time likely forgot within days.
