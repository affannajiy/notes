## In one sentence

Manhole covers are round mainly because a circle is the one shape that cannot fall through its own hole no matter how it is turned, which matters a lot when the cover sits directly over a hole people and equipment stand next to, and rolling a heavy disc is also far easier than dragging a heavy square.

## Explain it

Truth be told, this is a genuinely famous interview question, asked at Microsoft and Google in the 1990s and 2000s specifically to see how a candidate reasons through an everyday object, not because the actual answer is obscure engineering. A square or rectangular cover can be tilted on its diagonal and dropped straight down through its own opening, since the diagonal of a square is longer than its side, which means a square cover sized to sit flush on top can still slip through if angled correctly. A circle has no diagonal longer than its diameter in any direction, so a round cover can never be turned or tilted into an orientation that lets it fall through the same round hole it covers.

This in turn matters because a manhole cover falling into the hole below, often several metres deep with people or utility workers underneath doing maintenance, is a serious safety failure, not just an inconvenience. Circular covers also happen to be easier to manufacture, since they can be cast in a lathe or a simple round mould, and easier to move, since a heavy iron disc, often 90 kg or more, can be rolled on its edge by one person rather than dragged or carried the way a square slab would need to be.

## Example / non-example

- Example: standard utility manholes across most cities worldwide, including Malaysia's, use round cast iron covers for exactly this reason, and the hole beneath is cut round to match, so the cover and the opening are the same basic engineering decision made twice.
- Non-example: some access covers, particularly for shallow underground vaults or small utility boxes that nobody stands directly over, use square or rectangular covers instead, because the fall-through risk barely applies when the opening is small and shallow enough that a tilted cover has nowhere dangerous to go.

## Why it matters

So does a circle actually have to be the shape used, or could another shape work just as well? Mathematically, any shape of constant width, called a Reuleaux polygon (named after mathematician Franz Reuleaux, a curved shape with more than one straight side but the same width measured from any angle) shares the same "cannot fall through its own hole" property that a circle has, and a small number of manhole covers using such shapes do exist. In practice almost nobody bothers, because a circle is simpler to manufacture and reason about, and the extra mathematical elegance of a Reuleaux shape buys nothing a plain circle does not already provide.

In addition, the interview-question fame of this puzzle created a minor myth that round is the only safe shape, when the real underlying rule is about constant width, not roundness specifically. In our case, I find this a good example of an engineering answer that is simple enough to explain to a child (it cannot fall in) while the fuller mathematical version underneath (constant width shapes) is genuinely more interesting than most people asked the question ever get to.
