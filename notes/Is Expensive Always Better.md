## My take in one line

Price tracks cost, brand and scarcity far more reliably than it tracks actual quality, so expensive is better than cheap only within the same category made by people who care, and completely unrelated to quality the moment brand markup or artificial scarcity enters the price.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, "you get what you pay for" is one of those phrases repeated so often it starts to feel like a law of physics rather than a rough heuristic that happens to be true sometimes. I want to actually test it rather than just repeat it.

## The case for

Firstly, in categories with real engineering differences, price often does track quality closely. A 2013 blind taste test cited by Consumer Reports and separate wine studies found that professional wine judges could not reliably distinguish expensive wine from cheap wine in blind tastings, yet the same is not true for something like laptop batteries or camera sensors, where a cheaper component genuinely degrades faster or performs worse in ways that are measurable, not just perceived.

In addition, higher prices sometimes fund things that legitimately cost money: better materials, stricter quality control, longer warranties, or ethical labour practices, so a \$150 pair of shoes made in a factory with proper safety standards can be genuinely more durable and better made than a \$20 pair cutting every corner to hit that price.

## The case against

On the other hand, brand markup breaks the link entirely. A 2010 study by economist Robin Goldstein, referenced widely in behavioural economics writing, found no meaningful correlation between wine price and enjoyment among non-expert drinkers, and in some tests people rated cheaper wine higher once labels were hidden. Furthermore, luxury goods deliberately price above cost to signal exclusivity, an effect economists call a Veblen good, whereby demand can actually rise as price rises, because the price itself is the product being bought, not just the material good.

Moreover, "limited edition" pricing and hype-driven scarcity, sneaker resale markets being the clearest modern example, push prices far above any measurable quality difference, whereby a shoe identical in materials to a cheaper model sells for ten times more purely because fewer were made. Price here reflects supply and demand for status, not a better product underneath.

## Where I land

In my opinion, expensive is a weak, unreliable proxy for quality that works best in categories with visible engineering differences and fails badly the moment brand, scarcity or hype enters the price. In our case, I check reviews and specifications before price whenever I buy anything technical, since I have been burned once buying an overpriced cable that performed identically to one a tenth the cost, and once genuinely rewarded by paying more for a laptop with a battery that has outlasted three cheaper ones bought by friends in the same years.

(To which my father once said, buying yet another "premium" phone case, "you are not paying for the case, you are paying to feel like you did not buy the cheap one," which is probably the most honest answer anyone has given me on this whole question.)

## What would change my mind

A large blind study across many product categories, not just wine, showing price reliably predicting measured durability or performance even after controlling for brand recognition, would move me toward "usually true." Until then I treat price as one weak signal among several, not the main one.
