## My take in one line

Reviews are useful in aggregate and unreliable individually, since a large volume of reviews genuinely predicts a product's real quality reasonably well on average, while any single review, especially an extreme one, tells you more about that one reviewer's unusual experience or motive than about the product itself.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I have bought things with glowing five-star reviews that disappointed me and things with a mediocre 3.5 average that I ended up loving, which made me question whether I was reading reviews wrong or whether the review system itself was the unreliable part.

## The case for

Firstly, large-scale studies of online review platforms, including research on Amazon and Yelp data referenced in consumer behaviour journals, have found average star ratings across a large number of reviews do correlate meaningfully with actual product returns and satisfaction rates, suggesting the aggregate signal, given enough volume, is genuinely informative rather than noise. In addition, reviews solve a real information gap a buyer cannot close alone before purchase, whether a mattress is actually as firm as described, whether a restaurant's kitchen is clean, information a seller has no incentive to volunteer honestly on its own.

Furthermore, detailed written reviews, as opposed to the star rating alone, often surface specific defects or use cases, a laptop that overheats under heavy load, a blender that struggles with ice, that a product description would never mention and only daily use would surface.

## The case against

On the other hand, fake reviews are a large, documented problem: a widely cited 2021 Fakespot study and separate FTC research found a significant share of reviews on major platforms show patterns consistent with being purchased or incentivised, which the FTC has since begun fining companies over. Moreover, reviewer motivation skews the sample, since people with an extreme experience are far more likely to bother writing a review than someone with an ordinary one, so visible reviews over-represent the tails, not the typical middle.

So where does this go wrong most visibly? A single one-star review describing a defective unit can sit at the top of a listing and disproportionately shape a buyer's impression, even when the same product has hundreds of quietly satisfied five-star buyers who never bothered to write anything, which is exactly the sampling bias the aggregate rating is supposed to smooth out but any one review reintroduces immediately.

## Where I land

In my opinion, reviews are genuinely useful in aggregate, hundreds averaging to a reliable signal, and unreliable read individually, especially at the extremes, so the skill worth developing is reading the distribution rather than the headline star rating. In our case, I now check review count and the shape of the spread, five stars against one stars, rather than trusting the average alone, which has caught several products with a worrying cluster of one-star complaints about the exact same defect.

(To which a friend, scrolling past a product with 4.8 stars and eleven reviews, said, "that is not a rating, that is a rounding error," which is roughly the same point made more bluntly.)

## What would change my mind

Evidence that fake and incentivised reviews have grown common enough to meaningfully corrupt even large-volume aggregate ratings, not just small-sample or new-listing ratings, would push me toward trusting reviews far less even in aggregate.
