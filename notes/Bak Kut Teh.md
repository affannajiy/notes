## In one sentence

Bak kut teh is pork ribs simmered for hours in a dark herbal broth of star anise, cinnamon, cloves and dang gui, and despite the name literally meaning "meat bone tea" in Hokkien, the dish traces to Chinese labourers in Klang, Selangor, using medicinal herbs to help their bodies recover from physically brutal dock work, not to any actual tea in the pot.

## The story

Truth be told, the "tea" in bak kut teh refers to the strong Chinese tea traditionally served alongside the soup to help cut through the richness of the pork fat, not an ingredient in the broth itself, which is a detail that surprises most people ordering it for the first time. The dish is generally credited to Chinese immigrant dock workers in Klang in the early to mid 20th century, who needed a cheap, restorative meal built from inexpensive pork cuts, bones and offal, simmered with herbs believed in traditional Chinese medicine to build strength and ease joint pain from the physical strain of port labour.

Klang still fiercely claims the title of bak kut teh's birthplace, with several stalls tracing lineage back multiple generations and running long-standing disputes over which one served it first, similar in spirit to the origin arguments around [[Nasi Kandar]] and Penang.

## Why it is the way it is

Under the hood, the herbal mix, usually including star anise, cinnamon bark, cloves, dang gui (a root used in traditional Chinese medicine) and sometimes wolfberries, is simmered with pork ribs for hours until the broth turns dark and deeply savoury, closer to a medicinal tonic than an ordinary soup base. Firstly, this is why bak kut teh tastes noticeably different from other Chinese pork soups in Malaysia, since the herb blend, not just soy sauce or stock, defines the flavour.

Secondly, the dish is almost always served with fried dough sticks called youtiao, meant to be dipped into the broth to soak it up, and with rice on the side, since the soup itself is intensely rich and works better as an accompaniment to plain starch than as a standalone bowl. Thirdly, a dry version, "bak kut teh kering," exists too, where the same ingredients are cooked with much less liquid into a thick, almost stew-like coating instead of a soup.

## The variants

| Name | What changes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Klang-style | Dark, herbal, the original benchmark |
| Teochew-style (Singapore) | Peppery, lighter broth, less herb-heavy |
| Bak kut teh kering | Dry version, thick sauce instead of soup |
| Vegetarian bak kut teh | Herbs and mock meats, no pork |

## Where I met it

In our case, bak kut teh is a dish I associate specifically with early morning breakfast trips to Klang with extended family, since the best stalls there open before dawn and sell out by midday, which means the good bowl only exists for people willing to show up while everyone else is still asleep.
