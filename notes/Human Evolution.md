## In one sentence

Human evolution is not a single straight line from ape to modern human, it is a branching tree with many now-extinct human-like species along the way, and modern humans are simply the one branch, out of at least several that once coexisted, that happened to survive to the present day.

## What it is

Human evolution refers to the evolutionary process by which modern humans, Homo sapiens, gradually developed from earlier hominin ancestors over roughly six million years, shaped by natural selection acting on traits like brain size, bipedal walking and tool use as environmental pressures changed across that span.

## Types

Early hominins, Australopithecus among the most studied, walked upright but retained a brain size much closer to modern great apes, suggesting bipedalism, walking on two legs, evolved well before the dramatic brain size increase that came later. Archaic Homo species, Homo erectus and Homo neanderthalensis among them, developed larger brains, controlled fire and more sophisticated tools, and Neanderthals specifically are now known to have interbred with early Homo sapiens, meaning many people alive today carry small amounts of Neanderthal DNA. Modern Homo sapiens, emerging in Africa roughly 300,000 years ago based on current fossil evidence, eventually spread across every inhabited continent, a migration pattern still actively being refined through new fossil finds and genetic evidence.

## Terms

A few terms recur across human evolution discussion worth defining plainly. "Hominin," the broader group including modern humans and all our now-extinct close relatives and ancestors, is distinct from the even broader "hominid," which also includes living great apes like chimpanzees and gorillas. "Bipedalism," walking upright on two legs as the primary form of movement, is considered one of the earliest and most defining hominin traits, predating the large brain size often assumed to be evolution's main achievement.

## History in moments

The 1974 discovery of "Lucy," a roughly 3.2-million-year-old Australopithecus afarensis skeleton found in Ethiopia, gave scientists an unusually complete early hominin fossil, dramatically advancing understanding of early bipedal walking. Genetic sequencing of Neanderthal DNA, achieved for the first time in 2010, revealed conclusive evidence of interbreeding between Neanderthals and early modern humans, overturning the earlier assumption that the two groups never mixed at all. Ongoing fossil discoveries across Africa continue to push back the estimated date for the earliest Homo sapiens fossils, a 2017 discovery in Morocco dated to roughly 300,000 years ago having significantly revised the previously assumed timeline.

## Malaysian connection

Southeast Asia holds its own significant hominin fossil record, the "Hobbit" species Homo floresiensis discovered on the Indonesian island of Flores in 2003 being a striking regional example of a small-bodied human relative that survived until surprisingly recently in evolutionary terms.

## What's happening now

Ancient DNA analysis continues to reshape the human evolution story faster than fossil discovery alone ever could, revealing interbreeding events and population movements that leave no obvious trace in bones but remain clearly visible in genetic data recovered from surviving fossil remains.
