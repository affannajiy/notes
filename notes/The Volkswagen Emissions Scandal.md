## In one sentence

Volkswagen installed software in about 11 million diesel cars worldwide that detected when the car was being tested for emissions and switched to a cleaner mode only then, passing regulatory checks while emitting up to 40 times the legal limit of nitrogen oxide on the road, a fraud that cost the company over \$30 billion once it was caught in 2015.

## Situation

Volkswagen, one of the world's largest carmakers, had spent years marketing its diesel engines as "clean diesel", combining strong fuel efficiency with the ability to meet strict emissions standards, particularly in the US, where diesel emissions rules were tighter than Europe's. On 18 September 2015, the US Environmental Protection Agency issued a notice of violation against Volkswagen, revealing that the company had installed illegal software, later called a "defeat device", in diesel vehicles sold from 2009 onward. Volkswagen's share price fell by over a third within two days of the announcement, and CEO Martin Winterkorn resigned within the week.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Volkswagen's diesel engines could not actually meet US nitrogen oxide emissions limits while also delivering the fuel economy and performance the company had promised customers, and rather than accept a real tradeoff between the two, engineers built software that recognised the specific conditions of a laboratory emissions test, steady speed, no steering movement, and switched into a low-emissions mode only during that test, then reverted to a higher-emissions, higher-performance mode for ordinary driving.

## What happened

Firstly, the deception was discovered not by a regulator's own testing but by researchers at West Virginia University, funded by a non-profit clean transportation group, who tested Volkswagen diesel cars on actual roads rather than in a lab and found nitrogen oxide emissions far exceeding what the same cars showed in official lab tests, a gap that eventually led the California Air Resources Board and the EPA to investigate directly.

Secondly, once confronted with the data, Volkswagen initially offered only technical explanations before admitting to US regulators the software was a deliberate defeat device, turning a technical dispute into a criminal fraud case almost overnight.

Thirdly, Volkswagen ultimately admitted the defeat devices were installed in around 11 million vehicles worldwide, not just in the US, and the company pleaded guilty in the US in 2017 to conspiracy, obstruction of justice and other charges, while several executives, including one engineer, received prison sentences.

Lastly, the financial toll reached over \$30 billion by the early 2020s across fines, buybacks, recalls and settlements, and Volkswagen accelerated its shift to electric vehicles afterward, partly since "clean diesel" was a phrase the company could no longer credibly use.

## Lessons

To begin with, a technical shortcut built to satisfy a regulatory test rather than the actual real-world standard the test exists to measure is fraud dressed up as engineering, regardless of how sophisticated the software behind it looks. In addition, this case is now the standard reference whenever regulators discuss the limits of lab-based compliance testing generally, since it proved a determined company could game a controlled test environment for years before independent real-world testing caught the gap.

For me, I feel the detail that stands out most is that outside academic researchers, not the regulator's own routine testing, found the discrepancy first, which says something uncomfortable about how much a compliance system can rely on the honesty of the company being tested rather than independent verification.
