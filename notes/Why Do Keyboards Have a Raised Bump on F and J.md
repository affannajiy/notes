## In one sentence

The small raised bumps, called home row markers, on the F and J keys exist so a touch typist can find the correct hand position on a keyboard purely by feel, without ever looking down, since those two keys sit directly under the left and right index fingers in the standard touch-typing hand position taught alongside the QWERTY layout covered elsewhere in this vault.

## Explain it

Truth be told, most people notice these bumps at some point and assume they are just a manufacturing texture detail, until they actually learn to type without looking at the keyboard, at which point the bumps' real purpose becomes obvious immediately. Touch typing relies on a standard resting hand position, left hand fingers resting on A, S, D, F and right hand fingers resting on J, K, L, semicolon, a layout taught specifically so a typist's fingers can return to a known, consistent starting point after reaching for any other key.

F and J, sitting under the left and right index fingers respectively in that resting position, were chosen as the marked keys specifically because the index fingers are the ones doing the most reaching to other keys across the keyboard, and a typist needs a reliable, feelable anchor point to return to after each excursion. This in turn is why the bumps are raised rather than simply printed differently, a visual marking would be useless to someone deliberately not looking at the keyboard, while a raised, feelable texture works exactly as intended, letting a finger confirm correct positioning through touch alone.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a blind touch typist relies on these exact bumps as their primary, sometimes only, method of confirming correct hand placement before typing, showing the feature's design intent, tactile rather than visual confirmation, in its clearest possible use case.
- Non-example: number pad keys on many keyboards include a similar raised marker on the 5 key specifically, following the identical logic for a completely separate resting hand position used when entering numeric data quickly, confirming this is a general touch-input design principle, not a feature unique to the main alphabetic keys.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a keyboard curiosity? Firstly, this is a clear example of a design detail built entirely around a specific skill, touch typing, that assumes and rewards not looking at the keyboard at all, meaning the feature is nearly invisible in value to a hunt-and-peck typist while being genuinely essential to someone who has learned the touch-typing method properly.

In addition, this same principle of tactile anchoring shows up in other interface design contexts beyond keyboards entirely, physical buttons on game controllers, camera shutter buttons and certain car dashboard controls often include similar raised or textured markers specifically so a user's hands can operate them without shifting visual attention away from whatever else they are focused on, driving, filming, gaming.

In our case, the detail that stands out most is how small and easy to overlook this feature is relative to how genuinely load-bearing it becomes the moment a person actually relies on touch typing as their primary method, a reminder that a lot of thoughtful interface design hides in details most users never consciously register at all.
