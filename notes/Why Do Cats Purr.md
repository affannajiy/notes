## In one sentence

Cats purr through rapid, repeated twitching of the muscles around their voice box, roughly 25 to 150 times per second, and while purring clearly signals contentment much of the time, cats also purr when injured, in labour or dying, which has led researchers to think the behaviour may double as a low-level self-healing mechanism, not just an emotional display.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "purring means a cat is happy" is the answer most people learn and stop at, but it turns out to be only part of a more genuinely puzzling picture. Mechanically, purring comes from a cat's laryngeal muscles, the muscles surrounding the voice box, contracting and relaxing rapidly and rhythmically, a pattern controlled by a neural oscillator in the brain rather than by any single vocal cord movement, and that rapid muscle twitching modulates airflow during both inhaling and exhaling to produce the continuous sound.

What makes purring genuinely strange is that cats purr in contexts that have nothing to do with contentment, while giving birth, while severely injured, and even while dying, situations where a simple "happiness signal" explanation clearly does not fit. This in turn led researchers to a competing theory, that the specific frequency range cats purr at, roughly 25 to 150 hertz, overlaps with frequencies shown in some studies to stimulate bone density and tissue repair, meaning purring might function partly as a low-energy, low-cost mechanism supporting healing and pain management, alongside whatever emotional signalling it also does.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a cat purring loudly while being petted and relaxed fits the contentment explanation cleanly, and is genuinely the most common context purring occurs in, which is why that explanation became the default one most people know.
- Non-example: a cat purring steadily while giving birth, a context involving real physical stress rather than relaxation, does not fit a pure contentment explanation at all, which is exactly the kind of case that pushed researchers toward the self-healing hypothesis as an additional, not competing, explanation.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a cute fact? Firstly, veterinarians increasingly treat purring as an ambiguous signal rather than a reliable one, since a purring cat at the vet's office may be anxious or in pain rather than calm, meaning body language, ear position, tail movement, posture, matters more than purring alone for actually reading a cat's state.

In addition, this dual explanation is a useful reminder that a single observable behaviour does not have to serve only one function, purring plausibly does real emotional signalling work and real physiological work simultaneously, and treating it as purely one or the other undersells what is actually a fairly sophisticated evolved behaviour.

In our case, the detail I find most interesting is that cats are one of the only animals that vocalise continuously on both the inhale and the exhale, most mammal vocal sounds happen only on the exhale, which is part of why purring sounds so distinctly different from a growl, a meow or almost any other animal sound.
