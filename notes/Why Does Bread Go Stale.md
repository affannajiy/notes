## In one sentence

Bread goes stale mainly through a process called starch retrogradation, where the starch molecules that gelatinised and softened during baking slowly reorganise back into a firmer, more crystalline structure as bread cools and sits, and this happens even in sealed packaging, which is why staling is fundamentally different from drying out, despite the two often being confused.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "bread goes stale because it dries out" is the explanation most people default to, and it turns out to be largely wrong, or at least a smaller factor than the real mechanism. Bread's dough contains starch molecules bundled tightly together in their raw, uncooked state, and baking's heat causes those starch molecules to absorb water and unwind, called gelatinisation, which is what gives fresh bread its soft, tender crumb structure in the first place.

As bread cools after baking, those unwound starch molecules do not stay that way, they gradually realign and re-bond into a firmer, more ordered crystalline arrangement over the following hours and days, called retrogradation, and this is the dominant driver of staling's characteristic firmness and dryness in the mouth, not actual moisture loss from the loaf. This in turn is why sealing bread in an airtight bag slows staling only partially, retrogradation continues happening chemically inside the loaf's crumb regardless of whether moisture is escaping to the outside air at all.

## Example / non-example

- Example: bread left on a counter overnight, still inside its sealed bag, retaining nearly all its original moisture, can still feel noticeably firmer and less pleasant the next morning, confirming retrogradation, not moisture loss, drove the texture change.
- Non-example: bread frozen immediately after cooling and thawed later stays notably fresher than bread simply left at room temperature for the same duration, since freezing slows the specific molecular rearrangement retrogradation depends on far more effectively than sealed storage at room temperature does.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how bread gets stored or revived in practice? Firstly, this is exactly why briefly reheating stale bread in an oven can genuinely restore some softness, heat partially re-gelatinises the retrograded starch, temporarily reversing part of the same process that caused the staling, though the effect fades again as the bread cools back down.

In addition, this is why the refrigerator, often assumed to help preserve bread, actually accelerates staling rather than slowing it, since the temperature range inside most fridges happens to be close to ideal for retrogradation's molecular rearrangement, meaning bread stored in a fridge can go stale faster than bread left at room temperature.

In our case, the detail that reframes staling for me is that it is a genuine chemical transformation happening inside otherwise perfectly edible, perfectly moist bread, not simple evaporation, which is exactly why "just keep it sealed tighter" only ever solves part of the problem.
