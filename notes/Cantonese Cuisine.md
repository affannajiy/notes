## In one sentence

Cantonese cooking's defining principle is restraint, letting an ingredient's own natural flavour lead rather than burying it under heavy sauce or strong spice, which is exactly why the cuisine leans so heavily on fresh seafood, quick steaming and light stock-based soups over anything deep-fried or drowned in chilli.

## What it is

Cantonese cuisine is the cooking tradition of Guangdong province in southern China, built around freshness, light seasoning and precise timing, most visibly in dim sum, steamed fish and clear soups, and it reached Malaysia through generations of Cantonese migration into towns like Kuala Lumpur and Ipoh, where it now sits alongside other Chinese regional cuisines rather than standing apart from them.

## Types

Dim sum, the small steamed or fried dishes served in bamboo baskets alongside tea, is Cantonese cuisine's most globally recognised export, built specifically around variety and sharing rather than one large plate per person. Roasted meats, char siu, roast duck and soy sauce chicken hung in shop windows are a second major pillar, relying on a specific roasting technique and glaze rather than the steaming or stir-frying used elsewhere in the cuisine. Congee and clear soups form a third category entirely, built around long, gentle simmering to extract flavour from bones or dried ingredients without any of the heavier spicing found in, say, Sichuan cooking.

## Terms

A few terms recur across Cantonese food discussion worth defining plainly. "Wok hei," literally "breath of the wok," describes the specific smoky, slightly charred flavour a dish picks up from being cooked briefly over extremely high heat in a well seasoned wok, considered one of the clearest markers of a genuinely skilled Cantonese cook. "Yum cha," literally "drink tea," is the actual social ritual of eating dim sum, the tea itself traditionally considered just as central to the occasion as the food, which is why dim sum restaurants are so often simply called yum cha places instead.

## History in moments

Cantonese cuisine's global spread traces directly back to 19th and early 20th century migration out of Guangdong, with large Cantonese communities settling across Southeast Asia, Australia and North America and carrying their cooking traditions with them, which is a major reason Cantonese food, rather than any other Chinese regional cuisine, became many Western countries' default idea of "Chinese food" for much of the 20th century. Hong Kong's postwar rise as a major trading and financial hub in turn shaped Cantonese cuisine further, its dim sum culture in particular becoming closely associated with Hong Kong specifically even though the tradition's roots sit in mainland Guangdong.

## Malaysian connection

Cantonese cooking arrived in Malaysia through the same broad wave of southern Chinese migration that shaped much of the country's Chinese community, and it shows up clearly in dishes like Hainanese chicken rice's close cousin, roast duck rice, and in the char siu widely sold across Malaysian coffee shops, a lineage covered separately in this vault's own [[Hainanese Chicken Rice]] note on how migrant communities adapted regional Chinese dishes to local Malaysian ingredients and tastes.

## What's happening now

Cantonese restaurants worldwide, including in Malaysia, face rising pressure from a shrinking pool of chefs trained in the traditional multi-year apprenticeship system, a slow, hands-on training model increasingly at odds with a hospitality industry generally seeking faster, more standardised staff training.
