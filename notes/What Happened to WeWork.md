## In one sentence

WeWork was valued at \$47 billion in January 2019 by telling investors it was a technology company reinventing work, and by November 2019 that valuation had collapsed by over 80% once the same investors read the IPO paperwork and realised it was, underneath the branding, a company signing long, expensive leases and renting out desks at a loss.

## Situation

WeWork was founded by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey in New York in 2010, offering flexible co-working office space rented short-term to freelancers and companies, while WeWork itself signed long-term leases with landlords. It grew fast through the 2010s on funding from SoftBank and other investors, reaching a private valuation of \$47 billion by January 2019. In August 2019 WeWork filed paperwork to go public, and by that September, facing collapsing investor confidence, it withdrew the IPO entirely, Neumann was pushed out as CEO, and SoftBank had to inject a rescue package worth billions just to keep the company operating. WeWork eventually filed for bankruptcy protection in November 2023.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that WeWork's actual business, a real estate company subletting office space, is a low-margin, capital-intensive model that investors generally value modestly, while WeWork marketed itself as a technology company with software-style growth potential, a framing that justified a far higher valuation than the underlying leases and desks could support. The mismatch held up as long as private investors accepted the story; it broke the moment public market investors, during the IPO process, were required to actually read the financial filings.

## What happened

Firstly, the IPO prospectus, a legally required document disclosing the company's finances, revealed WeWork had lost about \$1.9 billion in 2018 alone, on revenue of about \$1.8 billion, meaning it spent nearly two dollars for every one dollar it earned, a ratio public investors were not willing to fund at the valuation WeWork wanted.

Secondly, the same filing exposed extensive related-party dealings by Neumann, including the company leasing office buildings that Neumann personally owned and paying him for the trademark to the word "We", details that raised serious governance concerns among the bankers underwriting the IPO.

Thirdly, once the IPO stalled, SoftBank, already WeWork's largest investor, had to choose between letting the company collapse, losing its earlier investment entirely, or funding a rescue, and it chose the rescue, injecting a package that valued WeWork at around \$8 billion, an 83% drop from January's \$47 billion in under a year.

Lastly, the core lease-versus-sublet mismatch never actually went away after Neumann left, and when COVID-19 emptied offices worldwide in 2020, tenants cut back on flexible space just as WeWork was still locked into the same long, expensive leases signed years earlier, which is what ultimately pushed the company into the 2023 bankruptcy filing.

## Lessons

To begin with, a company can be genuinely useful, and co-working space clearly was, without that usefulness justifying the valuation attached to it, since usefulness and business model soundness are different questions entirely. In addition, the disclosure requirements of going public exist specifically to force this kind of gap into the open, which is exactly what happened here.

For me, I feel the clearest lesson is that a business built on a long-term liability, the lease, funding a short-term, flexible product, the sublet, is fragile by construction, and no amount of branding around "community" and "the future of work" changes the mismatch sitting underneath it.
