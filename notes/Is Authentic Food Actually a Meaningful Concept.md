## My take in one line

"Authentic" almost always means "matches the version I first learned as the real one," which is a personal or generational benchmark, not a fixed historical fact, since every dish people now call authentic was itself a fusion or an adaptation at some earlier point that someone else once called inauthentic.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, writing the food notes in this vault kept surfacing the same pattern, [[Rendang]], [[Laksa]] and [[Cendol]] all have disputed origins and regional variants that different people insist is the "real" one, which made me suspicious that authenticity is doing less descriptive work than people assume when they invoke it.

## The case for

Firstly, dishes do have documentable histories, specific ingredients, techniques and regions that a version can meaningfully diverge from, so "authentic" is not meaningless as a comparative claim: a curry laksa made with condensed soup mix instead of an actual spice paste and coconut milk is genuinely a different, arguably lesser, execution of the dish's own established technique, not just a stylistic choice equal to any other.

In addition, food historians and cultural preservation bodies, including UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage programme, treat certain preparation methods as worth protecting specifically because commercial shortcuts or careless adaptation can genuinely erode a technique to the point where the dish's defining character is lost, which suggests there is a real, defensible core worth calling more or less authentic.

## The case against

On the other hand, nearly every dish people call authentic today is itself the product of migration, trade and adaptation from something earlier, [[Char Kuey Teow]] and [[Nasi Kandar]] both being adaptations of techniques and ingredients carried by immigrant communities into a new place, which means the "authentic" version people defend now was, at some earlier point in its own history, a departure from whatever came before it that someone else would have called impure.

Moreover, food scholars, including work referenced in journals of gastronomy studies, note that authenticity claims are frequently more about identity and belonging than about verifiable historical accuracy, whereby the "authentic" version usually just means the version the speaker's own family, region or generation grew up with, so two people from different regions of the same country can each sincerely believe the other's version is the fake one, since both are really defending their own memory rather than a documented original.

## Where I land

In my opinion, "authentic" is useful only as shorthand for "close to an established regional technique," and actively misleading the moment it gets used to declare one version the single correct answer, since food has never actually stopped moving and adapting long enough for any version to be a fixed, final original. In our case, I have stopped correcting people who cook a dish differently from how my own family does it, since I now assume both of us learned a real, valid regional variant rather than one of us having the fake version.

(To which my grandmother, hearing this argument, said her own version is simply correct and the discussion is over, which I suspect is exactly the instinct this whole take is trying to explain.)

## What would change my mind

Evidence that a specific, single, well-documented original truly exists for most widely eaten dishes, rather than multiple parallel regional developments as the historical norm, would push me toward believing authenticity is a real, single-answer property rather than a moving, personal benchmark.
