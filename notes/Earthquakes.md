## In one sentence

An earthquake is the sudden release of energy that has been slowly building up for years, sometimes centuries, along a fault line where two sections of the Earth's crust are locked against each other by friction, and the actual shaking a person feels is simply that stored energy travelling outward through the ground as waves.

## What it is

An earthquake occurs when stress accumulated along a fault, a fracture in the Earth's crust where two rock masses can move relative to each other, exceeds the friction holding those masses in place, causing a sudden slip that releases energy as seismic waves radiating outward from the point of rupture.

## Types

Tectonic earthquakes, by far the most common and destructive type, occur where the Earth's massive crustal plates grind against or collide into each other, the Pacific "Ring of Fire" being the most seismically active zone on the planet precisely because so many plate boundaries converge there. Volcanic earthquakes, generally much smaller, occur when magma moving underground fractures surrounding rock, often serving as an early warning sign of an impending volcanic eruption. Induced earthquakes, a smaller but growing category, are triggered by human activity, deep wastewater injection linked to oil and gas extraction being a well documented cause in several regions that otherwise have little natural seismic activity.

## Terms

A few terms recur across earthquake discussion worth defining plainly. "Magnitude," measured today mainly on the moment magnitude scale, quantifies the total energy released at an earthquake's source, a logarithmic scale where each whole number increase represents roughly 32 times more released energy, not merely a proportionally stronger shake. "Epicentre," the point on the Earth's surface directly above where the rupture actually began underground, is distinct from the "hypocentre," the actual point of rupture at depth, a distinction that matters because the same magnitude earthquake produces very different surface effects depending on how deep that rupture sits.

## History in moments

The 1906 San Francisco earthquake, estimated at magnitude 7.9, killed an estimated 3,000 people and destroyed much of the city, directly spurring the development of modern seismology and building codes designed specifically to survive future major shaking. The 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, at magnitude 9.5, remains the strongest earthquake ever recorded by modern instruments, a scale of energy release genuinely difficult to grasp intuitively. The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and resulting tsunami, killing over 225,000 people across multiple countries, drove a significant global investment in tsunami early warning systems specifically covering the region.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia sits on relatively stable ground compared to neighbouring Indonesia and the Philippines, but the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami still struck Penang's coastline, killing dozens, a reminder that geographic proximity to an active seismic zone can matter even without a country's own ground being especially earthquake-prone.

## What's happening now

Earthquake early warning systems, detecting an earthquake's initial, weaker waves and issuing an alert seconds before the stronger, more destructive waves arrive, have expanded significantly across Japan, Mexico and parts of the United States, a genuinely useful window even when it amounts to only ten or twenty seconds of advance notice.
