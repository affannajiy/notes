## In one sentence

Streaming replaced physical media and scheduled broadcast television with on-demand, internet-delivered video within about a decade, a shift driven less by any single company's cleverness and more by broadband internet finally becoming fast and cheap enough for millions of households to reliably stream video at once.

## Situation

Through the 2000s, watching a film or show meant either a scheduled broadcast slot on television or a physical disc, DVD or Blu-ray, bought or rented from a store. [[Netflix After the Pandemic|Netflix]] itself started in 1997 as a DVD-by-mail company, not a streaming one, and only added its streaming feature in 2007, initially as a minor bonus for existing subscribers rather than the core product. The core problem at hand for any would-be streaming company at that time was infrastructure, not content: home broadband speeds through most of the 2000s were often too slow and too unreliable for smooth video playback, so streaming's growth genuinely could not outrun the broadband rollout underneath it.

## Core problem

The one tension the whole industry had to resolve was that streaming needed two things to mature roughly together, broadband fast enough for households to stream reliably, and enough licensed or original content to make subscribing worth it, and neither alone was sufficient, a chicken-and-egg problem that took years, not months, to work through.

## What happened

Firstly, broadband speeds and household adoption rose steadily through the 2010s, and by the early 2010s streaming quality video had become reliable enough for Netflix's streaming service to overtake its own DVD business, a milestone reached around 2011 to 2012.

Secondly, Netflix's decision to commission original content, starting with House of Cards in 2013, changed streaming from a delivery method for existing television and film into a competing production studio in its own right, a move every major streaming competitor later copied.

Thirdly, traditional media companies, having initially licensed their content to Netflix, began pulling it back once they recognised streaming's growth, launching competing services of their own, Disney+, HBO Max and Peacock among them, through the late 2010s and into 2020, fragmenting what had briefly been a single, convenient library back into several separate subscriptions.

Lastly, the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the shift further, since cinema closures and lockdowns pushed both viewers and film studios toward streaming releases far faster than either would have moved otherwise, a period covered from Netflix's specific angle in [[Netflix After the Pandemic]].

## Lessons

To begin with, a technology shift that looks purely like a content or business model story, "on-demand beats scheduled TV," usually has an unglamorous infrastructure precondition underneath it that had to mature first, in this case broadband penetration and speed, without which the content side could not have mattered.

In addition, the fragmentation that followed streaming's early consolidation shows that a genuinely convenient period in a market, one service holding most of the content people wanted, is often a temporary transitional state rather than the market's natural resting point, since competitors big enough to reclaim their own content eventually will.
