## My take in one line

Physical media, DVDs, Blu-rays, CDs and vinyl, is genuinely dying as the default way most people consume film and music, but it is not disappearing entirely, since a real, growing minority now buys physical copies specifically because streaming's convenience comes with a form of ownership that turns out to be far weaker than it looks.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I gave away most of my DVDs years ago assuming streaming had made them pointless, and only started reconsidering that when a film I had streamed and enjoyed simply vanished from every service I subscribed to a year later, unavailable anywhere except used physical copies.

## The case for

Firstly, the sales numbers are unambiguous, physical disc and CD sales have fallen for close to two decades as streaming access replaced the need to own a copy for most casual viewing and listening, and DVD and Blu-ray shelves have shrunk or disappeared from most retail stores entirely. In addition, streaming genuinely solves the practical problems physical media always had, storage space, scratches, the need to physically swap a disc, at a price most people find easy to justify against owning a large personal collection.

Furthermore, an entire generation has now grown up with on-demand access as the default expectation, never having built the habit of buying media to keep, which suggests the shift is generational and structural, not merely a temporary fashion within an otherwise stable market.

## The case against

On the other hand, streaming access is licensed, not owned, and licenses expire or get pulled entirely when a studio's distribution deal with a platform ends, a rights negotiation fails, or a company simply decides a title no longer earns its place in the catalogue, meaning a film or show available today carries no real guarantee of being available next year. This in turn is exactly the risk physical ownership eliminates, a purchased disc keeps working regardless of what any platform decides later, which is why the small but persistent "physical media revival" movement online frames disc buying less as nostalgia and more as insurance against disappearance.

Moreover, vinyl records have had a genuine, sustained sales resurgence since the mid-2010s, not merely a rounding error, driven partly by collectors and partly by listeners who specifically want a tangible object and ritual around listening that a streaming app cannot replicate, showing physical media's appeal is not purely about access at all in every case.

## Where I land

In my opinion, physical media has permanently lost its role as the default way most people consume film and music, and that shift will not reverse, but it has settled into a smaller, durable niche built around people who specifically value permanence, collectability or ritual over convenience. In our case, the vanished film that started this reconsideration for me was, in hindsight, exactly the scenario the disc-buying minority has been warning about the whole time.

(To which a collector friend put it more bluntly, "I don't trust a company to keep paying for my movie's licence forever," which is really just ownership stated as a risk calculation rather than a preference.)

## What would change my mind

If streaming platforms began offering a genuinely permanent, purchase-once digital ownership option with enforceable guarantees against removal, rather than the current licensed-access model, the practical case for physical media as insurance would weaken considerably.
