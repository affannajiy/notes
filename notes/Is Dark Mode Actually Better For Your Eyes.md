## My take in one line

Dark mode helps in one specific, narrow way, less blue light and glare in dark rooms, and does little to nothing for the two things most people actually blame it for fixing, general eye strain and long-term eye health, which are driven far more by screen brightness, contrast settings and blink rate than by whether the background is black or white.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I switched most of my apps to dark mode expecting my eyes to feel noticeably better by the end of a long work session, and when they did not, I assumed I was doing something else wrong, until I actually looked into what dark mode does and does not change physiologically.

## The case for

Firstly, in genuinely dark environments, a bedroom at night for instance, a bright white screen against a dark room creates a large contrast that can cause real, immediate discomfort and glare, and dark mode measurably reduces that specific problem by lowering the overall light emitted, a real benefit in that context. In addition, dark mode does reduce blue light exposure somewhat, and there is reasonably good evidence blue light close to bedtime can suppress melatonin, the hormone regulating sleep, so evening dark mode plausibly helps sleep onset a little, separate from any eye strain claim.

Furthermore, for people with some forms of astigmatism, dark mode can reduce a specific visual distortion called "halation," where light appears to bleed around bright text on a dark background, making it a genuine accessibility improvement for that subset of users.

## The case against

On the other hand, the American Academy of Ophthalmology and several clinical studies have found no strong evidence dark mode meaningfully reduces general digital eye strain for the average user, since the dominant causes are reduced blink rate, poor brightness or contrast settings, and viewing distance, none of which dark mode addresses directly. A 2020 JAMA Ophthalmology study testing reading performance and fatigue between light and dark mode found no significant difference for most participants, and some findings suggest dark mode can increase reading time and errors for people without astigmatism, since black text on white is generally the higher-contrast, easier-to-read configuration.

Moreover, "blue light causes eye damage," a claim frequently used to justify dark mode as protective, is not well supported at ordinary screen brightness and viewing distances, per the AAO's own guidance, which distinguishes this from the separate, better supported claim about blue light's effect on sleep timing.

## Where I land

In my opinion, dark mode helps comfort in low-light settings and evening sleep timing a little, and is largely irrelevant to daytime eye strain or long-term eye health, better addressed by the boring basics: regular breaks, blinking more consciously, and adjusting brightness to match the room. In our case, my eye strain during long sessions dropped far more from a 20-minute break reminder than from any dark mode toggle I have tried.

(To which a friend defending dark mode purely on aesthetics said, "I don't care if it helps my eyes, it just looks better," which is a completely different, and honestly more honest, argument than the health claim usually offered.)

## What would change my mind

A larger study measuring long-term eye health outcomes, not just short-term reading fatigue, between habitual dark mode and light mode users over years, showing a real protective effect, would change how confidently I dismiss the eye health claim.
