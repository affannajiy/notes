## In one sentence

Twelve became a genuinely popular counting unit across many ancient cultures independently, from Egyptian hour division to Roman measurement, mainly because 12 divides evenly into more smaller whole numbers, 2, 3, 4 and 6, than 10 does, making it a more practically convenient base for everyday trading, measuring and dividing goods fairly among groups of people.

## Explain it

Truth be told, ten only divides evenly by 2 and 5, while twelve divides evenly by 2, 3, 4 and 6, a genuinely meaningful practical difference for ancient traders and craftsmen who regularly needed to split a batch of goods, eggs, bread loaves or fabric lengths, into equal fair shares among a varying number of buyers or workers without leftover fractions causing dispute. This practical divisibility advantage likely explains why "dozen" counting, grouping items in twelves, became such a widespread and independently recurring convention across many unrelated ancient trading cultures rather than any single culture's invention spreading outward from one origin point.

This in turn is why base-12 counting shows up so persistently across measurement systems inherited from history, twelve inches to a foot, twelve months to a year, itself connected to the twelve-hour division of day and night covered separately in this vault's own [[Why Are There 24 Hours in a Day]] note, and the enduring English trade term "dozen" itself, derived from the Old French "douzaine," meaning a group of twelve, reflecting just how embedded the number became specifically in medieval European commerce and measurement.

## Example / non-example

- Example: eggs are still commonly sold by the dozen today largely because that packaging convention was already firmly established well before the metric system's more recent base-10 push, and changing an already familiar, universally recognised unit carries real practical cost for very little genuine benefit.
- Non-example: the metric system, deliberately designed during the French Revolution specifically to replace older, inconsistent measurement units with a cleanly unified base-10 structure, represents a genuinely conscious rejection of base-12's older divisibility advantages in favour of the simpler, more universally teachable base-10 logic instead.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond an interesting counting convention? Firstly, this history is a genuinely useful example of how a mathematically superior solution for one specific purpose, base-12's superior divisibility for everyday fair sharing, does not automatically win out over a simpler, more broadly consistent alternative, base-10's easier universal teachability, once an entire measurement system needs to be standardised at genuinely massive scale.

In addition, the same practical divisibility logic behind the dozen also explains the less common but still occasionally used "gross," meaning twelve dozen, or 144, a unit that shows up specifically in old wholesale trading contexts where goods needed to be counted, packed and shipped in genuinely large uniform batches.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a dozen's persistence into the present day says less about any deep mathematical necessity and more about how strongly an already familiar counting convention can resist replacement, even once a more systematically consistent alternative, the metric system specifically, becomes broadly available.
