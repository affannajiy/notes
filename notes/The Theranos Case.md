## In one sentence

Theranos raised over \$700 million and reached a \$9 billion valuation on the promise that a single drop of finger-pricked blood could run hundreds of accurate lab tests on its own machine, a technology that never actually worked, and the company ran most of its real tests on standard commercial lab equipment while telling investors, regulators and patients otherwise.

## Situation

Theranos was founded in 2003 by Elizabeth Holmes, a Stanford dropout, on the promise of the Edison, a proprietary blood-testing device that claimed to run comprehensive diagnostic panels from a few drops of blood taken by finger prick, instead of the multiple vials a normal blood draw requires. By 2014 Theranos had raised roughly \$700 million from investors including media mogul Rupert Murdoch and education secretary Betsy DeVos's family, reaching a peak valuation of \$9 billion, and had partnered with Walgreens to offer testing in retail pharmacy locations across the US.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that the Edison device could not reliably perform the range or accuracy of tests Theranos claimed, so the company ran the large majority of patient samples on modified commercial machines from other manufacturers, sometimes diluting the tiny finger-prick samples to work with equipment designed for larger blood volumes, a workaround that introduced its own accuracy problems, while continuing to market the technology publicly as its own proprietary breakthrough.

## What happened

Firstly, in October 2015 Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published an investigation, based on former employees speaking anonymously, revealing that Theranos ran the overwhelming majority of its tests on commercial machines, not the Edison, and that the Edison itself produced inconsistent, sometimes dangerously inaccurate results for tests like potassium levels.

Secondly, the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services inspected Theranos's lab in 2016 and found deficiencies serious enough to pose "immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety", leading to the lab's operating licence being revoked and Theranos banning Holmes from owning or operating a lab for two years.

Thirdly, the Securities and Exchange Commission charged Holmes and former company president Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani with massive fraud in 2018, alleging they had deceived investors about the technology's actual capabilities, and Theranos dissolved that same year, its assets liquidated.

Lastly, Holmes was convicted on four counts of fraud in January 2022 and sentenced to over 11 years in prison, while Balwani was convicted separately and sentenced to nearly 13 years, among the most severe outcomes for corporate fraud in Silicon Valley's history.

## Lessons

To begin with, the board and investors around Theranos included genuinely accomplished people, former secretaries of state and defence among them, which is a reminder that reputation and connections are not evidence a technology actually works, and due diligence on the underlying science matters more than the credibility of the people vouching for it.

In addition, a private company facing little regulatory scrutiny before its product reaches real patients, as Theranos largely avoided until Walgreens partnerships put its tests in front of actual consumers, can cause real harm long before the fraud becomes visible to outsiders.

For me, I feel the detail that stays with me most is how long the deception held, over a decade, entirely because almost nobody with access to the actual lab results was willing or able to speak up until a journalist gave former employees a way to do it anonymously.
