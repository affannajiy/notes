## In one sentence

Sixty seconds make a minute because ancient Babylonian mathematicians built their entire number system around the base of 60 rather than the base of 10 most of the world uses today, and that ancient sexagesimal system happened to get passed down specifically through astronomy and timekeeping traditions that never fully switched over to the more familiar decimal system everyone uses for ordinary counting.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the Babylonians, in ancient Mesopotamia roughly four thousand years ago, used a number system based on 60 rather than 10, a choice that offered a genuine practical mathematical advantage, 60 divides evenly by a great many smaller numbers, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30 among them, making fractions and division noticeably easier to calculate by hand than a base-10 system allows, since 10 only divides evenly by 2 and 5.

This in turn is why 60 became deeply embedded specifically in astronomy and geometry, fields where the Babylonians made enormously influential early contributions, dividing a circle into 360 degrees and, later, subdividing an hour into 60 smaller units, minutes, and each minute further into 60 even smaller units, seconds, following the exact same base-60 logic consistently at every level. In addition, later civilizations, Greek and then medieval European astronomers especially, inherited and preserved this specific Babylonian time-division system directly through their own astronomical calculations, cementing it permanently into scientific and everyday timekeeping practice well before the more familiar base-10 decimal system became dominant for ordinary counting and currency during the following centuries.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a clock face still visibly divides its dial into 60 individually marked minute positions, a direct, still-visible legacy of the exact same ancient Babylonian base-60 counting system used for astronomical calculation thousands of years earlier.
- Non-example: the French Revolutionary government's late 18th century attempt to introduce a fully decimalised, base-10 system of time, with 10-hour days and 100-minute hours, was abandoned within just a couple of years, demonstrating how genuinely difficult it proved to displace the deeply entrenched base-60 convention once it had already become universally familiar.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond ancient mathematical trivia? Firstly, this history is a genuinely useful example of how deeply a single ancient civilization's mathematical preference can become permanently embedded into a system, timekeeping specifically, that the entire modern world still relies on today without most people ever questioning why.

In addition, this same base-60 legacy connects directly to why a full circle contains exactly 360 degrees rather than some rounder base-10 number, and to why an hour splits into 60 minutes just as a day splits into 24 hours, covered separately in this vault's own [[Why Are There 24 Hours in a Day]] note, both tracing back to the same underlying ancient system.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that the specific numbers structuring a person's entire daily schedule were never chosen for any modern practical reason at all, they are inherited directly from a mathematical convenience ancient Babylonian astronomers identified millennia before clocks as we know them even existed.
