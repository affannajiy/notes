## In one sentence

Frying makes food taste better mainly because hot oil reaches temperatures far above boiling water, triggering the Maillard reaction, a chemical process between amino acids and sugars that creates hundreds of new flavour compounds and a browned, crisp surface no boiling or steaming method can produce at a lower temperature.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "fried food just tastes better" is one of those claims that sounds like pure preference until you look at the actual chemistry underneath it, at which point it turns out to be a genuinely measurable, well understood effect, not just habit or marketing. Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius and cannot get hotter at normal pressure no matter how long it is heated, which caps how much browning and flavour development a boiled or steamed food can undergo. Oil, by contrast, is commonly used for frying at 160 to 190 degrees Celsius, comfortably above water's ceiling, and that extra heat is what unlocks the Maillard reaction, named after French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, who first described it in 1912.

The Maillard reaction happens when amino acids, the building blocks of protein, and reducing sugars are heated together, rearranging into new molecules that did not exist in the raw ingredient at all, producing the deep brown colour and the complex, roasted, nutty flavour notes associated with a good crust on fried chicken, a seared steak or fried noodles like [[Char Kuey Teow]]. This in turn is layered with a second effect specific to deep frying: as the food's surface moisture flashes into steam and escapes, it leaves behind a genuinely crisp, dehydrated shell, a textural contrast against a moist interior that the brain reliably registers as more appealing than a uniformly soft texture.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a piece of chicken boiled versus the same piece deep-fried develops almost no browning in the boiled version, since it never exceeds 100 degrees Celsius, while the fried version develops a deep golden crust loaded with Maillard compounds within minutes.
- Non-example: steaming vegetables, while a genuinely healthy cooking method, produces almost no browning or crisping regardless of cooking time, precisely because steam, like boiling water, cannot exceed 100 degrees Celsius, which is why steamed and fried versions of the same vegetable taste noticeably different despite using similar cooking durations.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how food gets cooked in practice? Firstly, this is why professional kitchens specifically pat ingredients dry before frying or searing, since surface water has to evaporate before the temperature at that surface can rise high enough to trigger real browning, meaning a wet piece of food effectively wastes cooking time just boiling off moisture instead of developing flavour.

In addition, this is the same underlying reaction behind toasted bread, roasted coffee and grilled meat, meaning the appeal of fried food is really one specific, high-heat application of a much broader flavour-building principle that shows up across nearly every "browned" food people describe as more flavourful than its unbrowned equivalent.

In our case, this is exactly why a stall's char kuey teow tossed hard over intense heat tastes different from the same ingredients cooked gently at home, the wok hei effect mentioned in that note is, at its chemical core, this same Maillard reaction happening faster and more intensely than a home stove typically allows.
