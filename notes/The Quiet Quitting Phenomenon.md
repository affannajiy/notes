## In one sentence

"Quiet quitting," a term that went viral in 2022, describes employees deliberately doing only the work their job formally requires and no more, and the phenomenon it named was less a new behaviour than an old one, setting boundaries around unpaid extra effort, that suddenly got a catchy label right as post-pandemic burnout and stalled wage growth made the underlying frustration widespread.

## Situation

Through the pandemic years, many employees had taken on extra hours, extra responsibilities and greater flexibility demands without proportional pay increases, often framed by employers as necessary sacrifice during an unusual period. By mid-2022, as workplaces normalised again, many employees who had absorbed that extra load found it had quietly become the new expected baseline rather than reverting back, without the corresponding pay or recognition increasing to match. The core problem at hand is that going "above and beyond" at work, long treated as a straightforward path to recognition and advancement, had become in many workplaces something expected as a default rather than rewarded as exceptional, breaking the informal exchange many employees felt they had implicitly agreed to.

## Core problem

The one tension driving this case is that "doing only what your job description requires" sounds, stated plainly, like a completely reasonable baseline, yet workplace culture in many industries had drifted toward treating that same baseline as underperformance, creating a real gap between what employees were formally asked to do and what they felt informally pressured to do anyway.

## What happened

Firstly, a TikTok video posted in mid-2022 popularised the specific term "quiet quitting," and it spread rapidly across social media and then into mainstream business press coverage within weeks, turning a longstanding, largely private frustration into an openly discussed, named phenomenon almost overnight.

Secondly, employer and management reactions split sharply, some framed it as a concerning decline in work ethic and commitment, while others, including some HR researchers, reframed it more sympathetically as a rational response to burnout and stagnant compensation rather than a moral failing.

Thirdly, surveys conducted through late 2022 and 2023 found a meaningful share of employees across multiple countries identified with the term, suggesting the sentiment behind it was genuinely widespread rather than a purely online phenomenon confined to social media discourse.

Lastly, the term itself faded from headlines within roughly a year, as most viral workplace terms do, but many of the underlying discussions it sparked, about boundaries, unpaid overtime and burnout, continued in less viral, more sustained forms afterward.

## Lessons

To begin with, a behaviour does not need to be new to become suddenly visible, "doing only your job description" long predates 2022, and what actually changed was that a catchy label gave a widely felt but rarely voiced frustration a shared vocabulary people could recognise themselves in.

In addition, viral workplace terms tend to compress a genuinely complicated set of causes, burnout, pay stagnation, shifting expectations, into one simple phrase, which spreads the idea fast but also makes it easy for the underlying nuance to get lost in whichever framing, lazy worker or exploited worker, best fits whoever is repeating the term.
