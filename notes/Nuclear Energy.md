## In one sentence

Nuclear power plants generate electricity through nuclear fission, splitting atomic nuclei to release enormous amounts of energy, and the technology's biggest obstacle to wider adoption has never really been safety statistics, which are strong by most measures, but public perception shaped disproportionately by three high-profile accidents relative to how safe the industry's actual operating record has generally been.

## What it is

A nuclear power plant generates heat by splitting uranium or plutonium atoms in a controlled chain reaction, and that heat boils water into steam that drives a turbine, meaning a nuclear plant is, at its core, still a steam turbine generator, the same basic principle behind most coal and gas power plants, just with fission rather than combustion as the heat source.

## Types

Most operating reactors today are light water reactors, using ordinary water both to cool the reactor and to slow neutrons enough to sustain the fission reaction efficiently. A newer category, small modular reactors, aims to build smaller, factory-manufactured reactor units rather than large custom-built plants, intended to reduce construction cost and time, though the technology remains in relatively early commercial deployment as of the mid-2020s. Nuclear fusion, a fundamentally different process fusing atoms together rather than splitting them, remains an active area of research rather than a deployed power source, despite decades of steady progress toward a genuinely net-positive reaction.

## Terms

A few terms recur across nuclear energy discussion worth defining plainly. "Meltdown" describes a reactor core overheating badly enough to damage or melt its fuel, the failure mode behind the Chernobyl and Fukushima accidents. "Half-life," the time it takes half of a radioactive material's atoms to decay, determines how long nuclear waste remains hazardous, ranging from a few years for some byproducts to thousands of years for others, a key factor behind the ongoing difficulty of long-term waste storage planning.

## History in moments

The world's first commercial nuclear power plant, at Obninsk in the Soviet Union, began operating in 1954, followed quickly by a wave of nuclear plant construction across the US, Europe and Japan through the 1960s and 1970s. The 1979 Three Mile Island accident in the US, the 1986 Chernobyl disaster in the Soviet Union and the 2011 Fukushima disaster in Japan, triggered by a major earthquake and tsunami, each significantly slowed public and political support for new nuclear construction in different regions and eras, despite differing enormously in actual severity and cause.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia has explored nuclear power as a long-term option for its energy mix but has not built a commercial nuclear plant, and public and political caution following the Fukushima disaster specifically has been cited as a factor slowing any concrete move toward adopting the technology domestically.

## What's happening now

Nuclear power is seeing renewed interest in several countries as a low-carbon energy source that, unlike solar and wind, provides continuous baseline power regardless of weather or time of day, and rising electricity demand from data centres and industrial electrification has added fresh momentum to nuclear investment discussions even in some countries that had previously moved away from the technology.
