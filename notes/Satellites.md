## In one sentence

Satellites underpin a surprising share of daily life, GPS, weather forecasting, internet access in remote areas, and the industry's biggest recent shift has been a move from a small number of large, expensive satellites in high orbit toward vast constellations of small, cheap satellites in low orbit, a change made possible almost entirely by the falling launch costs covered in this vault's [[Space Exploration]] note.

## What it is

A satellite is any object placed deliberately into orbit around a planet, and while the term technically covers everything from crewed space stations to tiny research cubesats, most operational satellites exist to perform one of a small number of jobs, communication, navigation, or Earth observation.

## Types

Communication satellites relay signals, television, internet, phone calls, between distant points on the ground. Navigation satellites, GPS being the best known constellation, broadcast precise timing signals that receivers use to calculate their own position, covered from the receiver side in this vault's [[How Does GPS Know Where You Are]] note. Earth observation satellites capture imagery and data used for weather forecasting, climate monitoring and mapping. A newer structural distinction matters as much as job type now, geostationary satellites sit in one high, fixed orbital position covering a large fixed area, while low Earth orbit constellations use hundreds or thousands of smaller satellites working together to cover the same ground with lower latency and cheaper individual units.

## Terms

A few terms recur across satellite discussion worth defining plainly. "Latency," the delay between sending and receiving a signal, is significantly lower for low Earth orbit satellites than geostationary ones, simply because the signal travels a much shorter physical distance, a key reason newer satellite internet services favour low orbit despite needing far more satellites to achieve full coverage. "Space debris," defunct satellites and fragments left in orbit, has become a growing concern as constellations grow larger, since a collision between two objects in orbit can generate a debris field that threatens other satellites for years afterward.

## History in moments

Sputnik, launched by the Soviet Union in 1957, was the first artificial satellite, and its successful launch triggered rapid satellite development by both Cold War superpowers through the following decades. The GPS constellation, developed by the US military and made available for civilian use from the 1980s and 1990s onward, transformed navigation, logistics and countless everyday applications relying on precise positioning. SpaceX's Starlink, launched from 2019 onward, pioneered large-scale low Earth orbit internet constellations at a scale far beyond earlier satellite internet attempts, made commercially viable specifically by the reusable rocket cost reductions covered separately in this vault.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia operates its own communication satellites and has expressed interest in satellite internet services to improve connectivity in rural and remote areas, an application particularly relevant given the country's geographically split peninsula and Borneo regions.

## What's happening now

The rapid growth of low Earth orbit constellations has intensified concern over both space debris accumulation and light pollution affecting ground-based astronomy, prompting ongoing international discussion over how to regulate an increasingly crowded orbital environment before it becomes genuinely unmanageable.
