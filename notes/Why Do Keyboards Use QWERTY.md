## In one sentence

QWERTY, the standard keyboard letter layout, was designed in the 1870s for mechanical typewriters, and the widely repeated claim that it was arranged specifically to slow typists down and prevent jamming is likely a myth, but the layout stuck regardless once it became the industry standard, a case of early lock-in outlasting the mechanical problem it may or may not have originally solved.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the "QWERTY was designed to slow you down" story is one of the most commonly repeated explanations for the layout, and it deserves more scepticism than it usually gets. Christopher Latham Sholes, credited with designing the layout for his 1870s typewriter, did rearrange letters partly with mechanical jamming in mind, early typewriters used levers that could physically collide and stick together if two nearby keys were struck in quick succession, so separating certain frequently paired letters had a real mechanical justification.

However, historical research into Sholes' actual design process suggests the layout was shaped at least as much by the practical needs of telegraph operators transcribing Morse code, who were among the typewriter's earliest customers, as by any deliberate slow-down strategy, and some frequently paired letters, like "er" and "th," were not actually separated the way the jamming story would predict, weakening that explanation on its own terms. What is much better established is what happened after the design existed, once typewriter manufacturers and typing schools standardised around QWERTY, switching to any alternative layout required retraining every typist and retooling every machine, a cost that grew larger, not smaller, the more widely QWERTY spread, regardless of whether a better layout existed.

## Example / non-example

- Example: the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard, designed in the 1930s specifically to reduce finger travel and improve typing speed based on English letter frequency, has never displaced QWERTY despite decades of claims it offers real efficiency gains, consistent with lock-in, not layout quality, being the dominant factor keeping QWERTY in place.
- Non-example: mobile phone keypads and some specialised input devices did adopt entirely different letter arrangements when the technology itself changed enough that no existing QWERTY-trained muscle memory applied, showing switching costs specifically, not any inherent unchangeable superiority of QWERTY, is what keeps the layout locked in on full keyboards.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond typewriter trivia? Firstly, QWERTY is one of the most frequently cited real-world examples economists use to explain "path dependence," the idea that early historical choices, even mediocre or accidental ones, can become effectively permanent once enough infrastructure, training and habit accumulates around them, a pattern that shows up well beyond keyboards, in technology standards, file formats and measurement systems alike.

In addition, this is exactly why alternative keyboard layouts, however measurably efficient in isolated studies, have struggled to gain meaningful adoption, the switching cost, retraining an entire population of touch typists, dwarfs whatever speed gain an individual might realise, a cost-benefit calculation that rarely favours change once a standard is this deeply entrenched.

In our case, the detail that reframes this story for me is that the popular jamming explanation being questionable does not actually weaken the more interesting lesson here, QWERTY's survival says less about 1870s engineering than about how difficult it is to displace a standard once enough of the world has already learned it.
