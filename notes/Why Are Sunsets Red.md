## In one sentence

Sunsets turn red and orange because sunlight has to pass through a much longer stretch of atmosphere to reach an observer when the sun sits near the horizon, and that extra distance scatters away nearly all the shorter-wavelength blue light along the way, as explained in this vault's [[Why Is the Sky Blue]] note, leaving mostly the longer red and orange wavelengths to reach the eye directly.

## Explain it

Truth be told, once you already know why the sky is blue, the sunset question turns out to be almost the same physics viewed from a different angle rather than a separate phenomenon needing its own explanation. During the middle of the day, sunlight travels through a relatively short vertical slice of atmosphere to reach the ground, and Rayleigh scattering, the effect where shorter wavelengths of light bounce off atmospheric molecules far more than longer ones, spreads blue light across the sky while most of the red and yellow light continues on largely undisturbed.

At sunset, the sun sits low near the horizon, and sunlight reaching an observer at that angle has to travel sideways through a far longer path of atmosphere, sometimes many times the thickness it travels through at midday, before arriving. This in turn means far more scattering happens along that longer path, and by the time the light reaches an observer's eye, most of the blue and even much of the green has already been scattered away in other directions, leaving a light that looks noticeably shifted toward red and orange, the wavelengths that survive the longer journey with the least loss.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a sunset over open, clean, dry air often produces a fairly mild orange, while a sunset following a dusty day or after a wildfire nearby can produce a much deeper, more saturated red, since extra particles in the air, not just gas molecules, scatter even more of the remaining shorter wavelengths, pushing the surviving colour further toward red.
- Non-example: sunlight viewed straight overhead at noon through that same short atmospheric path shows almost none of this shift, confirming that path length through the atmosphere, not the sun itself changing colour, drives the sunset effect.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually show up beyond a nice view? Firstly, this is why sunsets are noticeably more dramatic and vivid after volcanic eruptions or major wildfires, since fine ash and smoke particles suspended high in the atmosphere scatter light even more strongly than air molecules alone, a documented effect after large eruptions like Krakatoa in 1883, which produced famously vivid skies worldwide for months afterward.

In addition, the same principle explains why the sun itself can be looked at more comfortably near sunset than at midday, not because it emits less light, but because so much of the light along that longer path has already scattered away before reaching the eye, though this is not a safe basis for staring at the sun directly regardless of the hour.

In our case, the detail that ties both notes together neatly is that the sky's blue and the sunset's red are not two separate stories, they are the same scattering effect measured at two different path lengths through the same atmosphere.
