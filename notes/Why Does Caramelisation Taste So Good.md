## In one sentence

Caramelisation is what happens when sugar alone is heated past about 160 degrees Celsius and breaks down into hundreds of new flavour compounds, distinct from the Maillard reaction covered in this vault's [[Why Does Frying Make Food Taste Better]] note, which needs both sugar and protein together, so caramelisation is really the "sugar-only" half of the same broader family of browning reactions that make cooked food taste more complex than its raw ingredients.

## Explain it

Truth be told, people often use "caramelised" loosely to describe any browned, sweet-tasting food, including onions and meat, which technically involves the Maillard reaction working alongside caramelisation rather than caramelisation alone, since onions and meat both contain protein. True caramelisation in the strict chemical sense only needs sugar and heat, no protein required, which is why plain white sugar melted in a dry pan turns from clear to golden to deep amber purely on its own.

As sugar heats past its melting point and continues climbing toward 170 to 180 degrees Celsius, the sucrose molecules break apart and recombine into new compounds, some bitter, some nutty, some fruity, in a cascading chain of hundreds of individual reactions that food chemists have only partially mapped even now. This in turn is why caramel tastes nothing like plain sugar despite starting as pure sugar, the sweetness that defined the raw ingredient gets partially replaced by an entirely new flavour profile that did not exist before the heat was applied.

## Example / non-example

- Example: caramelised onions, cooked low and slow for 30 minutes or more, develop a deep sweetness and brown colour through a combination of caramelisation of the onion's natural sugars and Maillard browning of its proteins working together, which is why they taste so different from a quickly sautéed onion cooked for only a few minutes.
- Non-example: sugar dissolved in cold water, however sweet, undergoes no caramelisation at all, since the reaction specifically requires heat well above body temperature, confirming that caramelisation is a heat-driven chemical transformation, not simply a property sugar carries by default.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how food gets cooked, beyond dessert? Firstly, this is why a barely-there golden crust on a crème brûlée's sugar top, made with a kitchen torch or broiler specifically to caramelise just the surface layer, tastes so different from the same sugar simply stirred into the custard underneath, since only the torched layer has actually undergone the reaction.

In addition, caramelisation is temperature-sensitive in a way that punishes carelessness: sugar can go from perfectly caramelised to burnt and bitter within a narrow window of thirty seconds to a minute on high heat, since the same reaction that produces pleasant nutty and toffee notes keeps progressing into acrid, burnt compounds if left too long, which is why professional pastry chefs treat caramel as one of the more failure-prone basics to master rather than an easy one.

In our case, the detail that changed how I think about this is that caramelisation and the Maillard reaction are doing genuinely different chemical work even though most cooked, browned food involves both happening together, which means "why does browned food taste better" actually has two separate, only partly overlapping answers depending on whether protein is present.
