## In one sentence

Wi-Fi's range problem comes down mostly to a trade-off most people never realise they are making, the faster, higher-frequency Wi-Fi bands that carry the most data also travel the shortest distance and struggle hardest to pass through walls, meaning a router advertising blazing speed is very often quietly sacrificing exactly the range a large home actually needs.

## Explain it

Truth be told, Wi-Fi routers typically broadcast on two or three different radio frequency bands, commonly 2.4 gigahertz, 5 gigahertz and increasingly 6 gigahertz, and a basic rule of radio physics applies consistently across all of them, higher frequency waves carry more data but travel shorter distances and get absorbed or blocked more easily by walls, furniture and even human bodies standing in the way. This in turn is exactly why the older 2.4 gigahertz band, despite being noticeably slower, still reaches significantly further through a house and penetrates walls better than the faster 5 or 6 gigahertz bands most modern devices prefer to connect on automatically for higher speed.

In addition, Wi-Fi signal quality is degraded further by interference from other devices sharing the same crowded frequency spectrum, microwave ovens, cordless phones and neighbouring routers all competing for the same limited 2.4 gigahertz band especially, alongside physical obstacles like metal appliances, thick concrete walls and even fish tanks, which absorb radio waves unusually well due to the water inside them.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a router placed in a home office often provides excellent signal in that room and the one next to it, but a bedroom two floors up and on the opposite side of the house frequently gets a noticeably weaker connection, precisely because each additional wall and floor between the router and device absorbs some of the signal's strength along the way.
- Non-example: an ordinary Bluetooth connection, covered separately in this vault's [[Why Is Bluetooth Called Bluetooth]] note, deliberately trades even more range for lower power consumption, working reliably only across a much shorter distance than Wi-Fi typically needs to cover.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond frustration over a weak signal in one room of a house? Firstly, this exact trade-off is why mesh Wi-Fi systems, using multiple coordinated access points spread through a home rather than relying on a single router, have grown popular, since adding physical access points close to where the signal actually needs to reach solves the range problem far more reliably than simply buying a supposedly more powerful single router.

In addition, understanding this frequency trade-off is genuinely useful for troubleshooting everyday connectivity, switching a device manually to the 2.4 gigahertz band when far from the router, rather than trusting automatic band selection, can noticeably improve a weak connection even though it means accepting a slower maximum speed in exchange for that improved range.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that Wi-Fi range and Wi-Fi speed are genuinely opposing goals given the same underlying radio physics, which is exactly why no single router setting or model can ever maximise both at once inside a typical home.
