## In one sentence

Satay is small pieces of marinated meat grilled on skewers over charcoal and dipped in a peanut sauce, and the format itself, small enough to cook fast on a stick, is generally traced to Javanese street vendors adapting Middle Eastern kebab-style grilling to Indonesian spices in the 19th century, which is why satay reads as unmistakably Southeast Asian despite the grilled-meat-on-a-stick idea existing worldwide already.

## The story

Truth be told, satay's spread from Java across the Malay Peninsula and into Singapore and Thailand happened so thoroughly that most Malaysians think of it as fully local, the same way many think of [[Rendang]] as purely Malaysian rather than Minangkabau in origin. Food historians generally credit the dish's spread to Javanese and other Indonesian immigrant traders through the 19th and 20th centuries, carrying a format that travelled easily since skewered meat needs no plates and cooks fast enough to sell one stick at a time on a street corner.

Satay has since become one of the dishes both Malaysia and Indonesia present internationally as a national food, and it appears regularly on global "best food" rankings, including CNN Travel's, which is part of why the same cross-border pride dispute that shows up with rendang shows up here too, without either country fully winning the argument.

## Why it is the way it is

Under the hood, the meat, usually chicken, beef or mutton, is cut into small cubes and marinated in a turmeric-heavy spice paste before skewering, and turmeric is doing real work here beyond colour, since it also acts as a natural preservative and gives the characteristic yellow-gold hue that separates satay visually from other grilled skewer dishes worldwide.

Firstly, the small cube size is deliberate, not just tradition, since a full-size cut of meat would char on the outside long before cooking through over an open charcoal fire, while small cubes cook fast enough to stay juicy. Secondly, the peanut sauce, made from ground roasted peanuts, sugar, chilli and usually a little tamarind for tang, is arguably as central to the dish as the meat itself, since a bad or thin peanut sauce is the fastest way for an otherwise well-grilled satay to disappoint.

## The variants

| Name | What changes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Satay ayam (chicken) | The most common everyday version |
| Satay daging (beef) | Richer, chewier, common at bigger gatherings |
| Satay kajang | From the town of Kajang, Selangor, famous specifically for its version |
| Sate Padang (Indonesia) | Thick, spiced gravy instead of peanut sauce, distinct regional style |

## Where I met it

In our case, satay for me is a night market food more than a home-cooked one, bought a few sticks at a time from a vendor fanning charcoal by hand, and I have never once seen the appeal of ordering it any other way, since the smoke and the wait in line are half of what makes it good.
