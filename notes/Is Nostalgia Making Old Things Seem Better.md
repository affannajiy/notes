## My take in one line

Nostalgia genuinely distorts memory in a measurable, one-directional way, smoothing out the boring and unpleasant parts of the past while keeping the good parts vivid, so old things do not just feel better because they were objectively better, they feel better partly because memory itself has already quietly edited them.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I once rewatched a childhood cartoon I remembered as brilliant and found it genuinely mediocre, which was uncomfortable enough that I wanted to understand whether my memory had actually changed the show or whether I had simply grown pickier with age.

## The case for

Firstly, some older things genuinely were better by objective measures, build quality on certain older appliances is measurably more durable than modern equivalents built to a lower price point, so "old things were better" is sometimes a defensible claim about specific products, not pure sentiment. In addition, psychologists studying nostalgia, including Constantine Sedikides and Tim Wildschut, have found nostalgic reflection carries genuine psychological benefits, boosting mood and a sense of meaning, a real emotional function worth taking seriously on its own terms.

Furthermore, cultural and technical context genuinely shifts what counts as good, so a comparison across eras is rarely apples to apples in the first place, meaning some of what reads as "old things were better" reflects a real difference in what was being optimised for, not just biased memory.

## The case against

On the other hand, a well documented phenomenon called "rosy retrospection," studied by psychologist Terence Mitchell and colleagues, found that people systematically rate past experiences more positively in memory than they rated the same experience while it was actually happening, based on comparing real-time and retrospective ratings of the same trips and events. This is a measured, repeatable bias, not just an impression, and it applies specifically to memory's own editing process rather than to any actual change in the thing remembered.

Moreover, media and products people fondly recall are usually a filtered, survivorship-biased sample to begin with, since most old films, songs and products from any decade were mediocre and have simply been forgotten, while the excellent fraction gets replayed and remembered, creating an illusion that "everything back then was better" when only the best surviving fraction is being compared against today's full range.

## Where I land

In my opinion, nostalgia is real, psychologically useful, and also a genuine source of comparison bias, so "old things were better" deserves scrutiny every time rather than automatic acceptance, since it is often true only because memory smoothed the bad parts away and survivorship bias already filtered out everything mediocre from that era. In our case, rewatching that childhood cartoon taught me to treat my own fond memories as a starting hypothesis worth testing against the actual old thing again, not as settled fact.

(To which my father, defending an old car he insists ran better than anything made today, said, "you weren't there for the breakdowns," which is rosy retrospection demonstrated in one sentence by the person committing it.)

## What would change my mind

Evidence that rosy retrospection effects shrink to negligible size once controlling for genuine survivorship bias in what gets remembered and compared, rather than nostalgia itself doing most of the distorting work, would push me toward believing "old things were better" reflects real quality differences more than memory bias.
