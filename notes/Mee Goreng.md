## In one sentence

Mee goreng is fried yellow noodles cooked with a sweet, tangy, tomato-and-chilli-heavy sauce, tofu, potato and a fried egg, and the specific "mamak" style most Malaysians picture, sweeter and saucier than similar fried noodle dishes elsewhere in Southeast Asia, comes directly from the same Indian Muslim mamak stall tradition already covered in this vault's [[Nasi Kandar]] and [[Teh Tarik]] notes.

## The story

Truth be told, "mee goreng" simply means "fried noodles" in Malay, a generic enough name that dozens of unrelated fried noodle dishes across the region technically qualify, but in Malaysia the phrase has come to mean one specific mamak-stall version strongly enough that ordering "mee goreng" without qualification almost always gets you the tomato-based version rather than any other fried noodle dish on the same menu.

The dish likely developed the way [[Teh Tarik]] and [[Nasi Kandar]] did, Indian Muslim hawkers adapting noodle cooking, itself originally a Chinese technique introduced to the region earlier, into something distinctly their own using tomato sauce, chilli paste and the potato and tofu that mark mamak mee goreng apart from Chinese-style fried noodle dishes like [[Char Kuey Teow]].

## Why it is the way it is

Under the hood, the defining ingredient is tomato sauce or a tomato-chilli paste, cooked into the noodles rather than added as a garnish afterward, which gives mamak mee goreng its distinctive reddish-orange colour and a sweet, slightly tangy flavour profile that separates it sharply from the darker, soy-based fried noodle dishes most other communities in Malaysia cook.

Firstly, potato cubes, usually pre-fried or boiled until slightly soft, are a near-universal addition specific to this style, adding a starchy bite that plain noodles alone would not have, and their presence is often used as a quick visual test for whether a mee goreng is being made properly mamak-style or not. Secondly, the noodles themselves are typically the same yellow, wheat-based noodles used across many Malaysian noodle dishes, tossed hard on a flat griddle at high heat, which lets the sauce cling and slightly caramelise onto the noodles rather than sitting as a separate wet coating.

## The variants

| Name | What changes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mee goreng mamak | The classic tomato-based, potato-and-tofu version |
| Mee goreng basah | A "wet" version with extra gravy poured over |
| Maggi goreng | Made with instant noodles instead of fresh yellow noodles |
| Mee goreng Jawa (Indonesia) | A related but distinct sweeter, kecap manis-based version |

## Where I met it

In our case, mee goreng is the dish I default to at any mamak stall when nothing else on the menu is calling to me, since it is reliably available everywhere and rarely disappoints, which makes it less exciting to write about than a dish I actively seek out, but also the honest, most-eaten answer if I am being truthful about frequency rather than fondness.
