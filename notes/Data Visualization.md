## In one sentence

Data visualization exists because the human brain is far faster at spotting a pattern in a shape than in a column of numbers, and the entire discipline is really about choosing the shape that reveals the true pattern in the data honestly, rather than the shape that merely looks the most impressive.

## What it is

Data visualization is the practice of representing numerical or categorical data visually, through charts, graphs, maps and diagrams, so that patterns, trends and outliers become easier for a human viewer to notice and understand than they would be reading the raw numbers directly.

## Types

Line charts suit data that changes continuously over time, a stock price or a temperature reading tracked across days, since the connecting line itself visually communicates the trend's direction and speed. Bar charts suit comparing discrete categories against each other, sales across different regions for instance, where each bar's height gives a direct, easily compared measure. Maps suit data that is inherently geographic, a "choropleth" map shading regions by intensity of some measured value being a common example, letting a viewer see spatial patterns a table of numbers by region name could never reveal as quickly.

## Terms

A few terms recur across data visualization discussion worth defining plainly. A "truncated axis," where a chart's vertical axis starts above zero rather than at it, can make a genuinely small difference between values look dramatically larger than it actually is, a technique used both innocently for readability and, less innocently, to exaggerate a point. "Chart junk," a term coined by statistician Edward Tufte, describes unnecessary decorative elements, three-dimensional effects, heavy gridlines, that add visual clutter to a chart without adding any actual information, actively making the underlying data harder to read clearly.

## History in moments

William Playfair, a Scottish engineer, is widely credited with inventing several now-standard chart types, the line graph and the bar chart among them, in his 1786 book on economic data, establishing visual conventions still in everyday use more than two centuries later. Florence Nightingale's 1858 "coxcomb" diagram, visualising mortality causes among British soldiers, is often cited as an early example of data visualization used deliberately to persuade policymakers rather than merely to record information. The rise of interactive, web-based visualization tools through the 2010s, letting viewers filter and explore a dataset themselves rather than see only a single fixed static chart, marked a genuine shift in how data gets communicated publicly.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysian government agencies have increasingly published interactive dashboards for public data, Covid-19 case tracking during the pandemic being a widely used recent example, reflecting a broader global shift toward making raw government data genuinely explorable by ordinary citizens rather than locked away in dense reports.

## What's happening now

AI tools are increasingly able to generate a chart directly from a plain-language description of what a user wants to see, a shift that raises real concern among data professionals about whether the underlying chart choice is actually appropriate for the data, since an easily generated chart is not automatically an honest one, a concern that connects directly to this vault's [[Why Percentages Can Be Deceptive]] note on how easily numbers get framed misleadingly.
