## In one sentence

Cryptocurrency went from a 2008 whitepaper written under a pseudonym, promising money nobody controls except the network of users running it, to a multi-trillion-dollar asset class traded by governments and pension funds, and the single idea that made the whole thing possible was solving a problem computer scientists had failed to crack for decades, how to stop the same digital token from being spent twice without a bank in the middle checking.

## Situation

In October 2008, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto, whose real identity remains unconfirmed to this day, published a nine-page whitepaper describing Bitcoin, a digital currency that needed no bank or government to issue or verify it. The first Bitcoin transaction happened in January 2009. For years cryptocurrency stayed a niche technical curiosity, until a mix of retail speculation, institutional adoption and thousands of competing coins pushed the total cryptocurrency market above \$3 trillion at its peak in November 2021, before a severe crash through 2022 wiped out a large share of that value, followed by a further recovery and mainstream institutional entry through the mid 2020s, including the approval of US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds in January 2024.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand, solved by Bitcoin's design, is the "double-spend problem": any purely digital token can in principle be copied and spent twice, since digital information is trivially duplicable, which is exactly why online money before Bitcoin always needed a trusted central party, a bank or a payment processor, to check and prevent that. Nakamoto's proposed fix was the blockchain, a public, shared ledger, meaning a record book, maintained not by one company but by thousands of independent computers worldwide that all have to agree on every transaction before it is accepted, removing the need for the single trusted party entirely.

## What happened

Firstly, Bitcoin's blockchain used a process called mining, whereby computers compete to solve a computational puzzle to add the next block of transactions, rewarded with newly created Bitcoin, which both secured the network against fraud and created the currency's supply at the same time, an elegant, if extremely energy-intensive, solution.

Secondly, once Bitcoin proved the concept worked, thousands of alternative cryptocurrencies followed, most notably Ethereum in 2015, which extended the blockchain idea beyond simple payments into "smart contracts", self-executing code running on the same trustless network, opening the door to decentralised finance, non-fungible tokens and a wider speculative ecosystem well beyond currency alone.

Thirdly, adoption swung between speculative retail bubbles, the 2017 and 2021 price surges among them, and institutional legitimisation, culminating in the 2024 US spot Bitcoin ETF approvals, which let mainstream investors hold Bitcoin exposure through ordinary brokerage accounts without directly managing cryptocurrency wallets themselves.

## Lessons

To begin with, a genuinely novel technical solution, the double-spend fix here, can unlock enormous speculative and financial activity long before anyone fully agrees on what the technology is actually for, currency, an investment asset, or something else entirely, and cryptocurrency's identity crisis on that question persists to this day.

In addition, the same trustlessness that makes cryptocurrency resistant to central control also removes the safety nets a traditional financial system provides, no deposit insurance, no simple fraud reversal, which is why crypto's biggest scandals, from lost exchange funds to outright fraud, have repeatedly hurt retail investors who assumed protections that were never actually built into the system.
