## In one sentence

BlackBerry owned the smartphone market by being the best physical keyboard and the most secure email in the world, and it kept building exactly that after the iPhone made touchscreens and app stores the thing people actually wanted, which is why a company with 20% of the entire mobile phone market in 2009 sold under 1 million phones a year by 2016.

## Situation

Research In Motion, a Canadian company founded in Waterloo, Ontario in 1984, launched the first BlackBerry device in 1999 and built its business on push email delivered instantly to a physical keyboard, which made it the standard device for corporate and government users worldwide through the 2000s. At its peak in 2011 BlackBerry had around 85 million subscribers and roughly 20% global smartphone market share as of 2009. By 2016 that had fallen to under 0.1% of the market, and BlackBerry exited making its own phones entirely in 2016, licensing the name to other manufacturers.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that BlackBerry's core strength, a tactile keyboard built for typing emails fast, and its greatest asset, enterprise-grade security, became irrelevant to the question smartphones started answering after 2007: could this device also be a good pocket computer for everything else, apps, browsing, media, games. Apple's iPhone, launched in 2007, and Google's Android, launched commercially in 2008, both bet the whole device on a touchscreen and an app ecosystem, a bet BlackBerry's leadership publicly doubted for years.

## What happened

Firstly, BlackBerry co-CEO Jim Balsillie reportedly dismissed the iPhone internally as no real threat to the enterprise market, believing corporate buyers would always prioritise the keyboard and battery life BlackBerry offered. This underestimation delayed any serious touchscreen response for years.

Secondly, when BlackBerry did respond, the 2013 BlackBerry 10 platform and the touchscreen Z10 phone arrived six years after the iPhone, with a smaller app catalogue than iOS or Android had already built, so it launched into a market that had already decided the format war was over.

Thirdly, the app ecosystem gap compounded itself: developers built for iOS and Android first because that is where the users were, which meant BlackBerry users had fewer apps, which meant fewer new users chose BlackBerry, a cycle the company never broke out of.

Lastly, in September 2013 BlackBerry wrote off \$1 billion in unsold Z10 inventory and reported a corresponding quarterly loss, a public admission that the pivot had failed, and the company shifted toward enterprise software and device licensing rather than competing as a phone maker.

## Lessons

To begin with, being the best at the previous generation's most important feature is not protection against a new generation redefining what the important feature even is. BlackBerry's keyboard and security were genuinely superior, and that superiority stopped mattering once the competition was being judged on apps and touch instead.

In addition, dismissing a competitor's product because it is worse at your core strength is a trap, since Balsillie was right that the first iPhone had a worse keyboard and less enterprise security, and wrong that those gaps would matter enough to stop it.

For me, I feel the clearest warning here is how long the gap was between the threat appearing in 2007 and BlackBerry's real response in 2013, six years is not a stumble, it is a company that convinced itself the ground was not actually moving.
