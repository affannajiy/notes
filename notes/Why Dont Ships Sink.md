## In one sentence

A steel ship floats not because steel itself is light, it is far denser than water, but because a ship's overall shape displaces a large enough volume of water that the combined weight of the ship and everything inside it stays less than the weight of water that volume would otherwise occupy, the principle of buoyancy first described mathematically by Archimedes over two thousand years ago.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "steel is heavier than water, so how does a steel ship float" is a genuinely fair question, and the answer has nothing to do with the material's own density in isolation. Archimedes' principle states that any object submerged in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces, and whether an object floats or sinks depends entirely on comparing the object's total weight against the weight of water its shape pushes out of the way.

A solid block of steel sinks because its shape displaces relatively little water for its weight, but a ship's hull is built hollow, a large enclosed volume of air sits inside a comparatively thin steel shell, and that hollow shape means the ship displaces a much larger volume of water than the same mass of solid steel ever would. This in turn is why a ship's overall average density, its total weight divided by the total volume its hull occupies including all that internal air space, ends up lower than water's density, even though the steel itself remains denser than water throughout, and it is this average, not the material, that determines whether the whole structure floats.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a small solid steel ball dropped into water sinks immediately, since its shape displaces very little water relative to its weight, while the same mass of steel shaped into a hollow bowl floats readily, demonstrating that shape and displaced volume, not material density alone, determine floating.
- Non-example: a ship that takes on water through a hull breach eventually sinks once enough water fills its previously air-filled internal space, since flooding replaces the light air that made the hull's average density low with heavy water, pushing the ship's overall density above water's and eliminating the buoyancy advantage its hollow shape once provided.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a physics curiosity? Firstly, this exact principle is why ship design pays enormous attention to internal compartmentalisation, dividing a hull into separate watertight sections specifically so a breach in one area floods only that section rather than the ship's entire internal air space, preserving enough buoyancy elsewhere to keep the vessel afloat.

In addition, this is why a ship's maximum safe cargo load is calculated so carefully, adding weight increases the ship's total mass without changing its hull's displaced volume, meaning there is a hard limit past which the ship's average density would exceed water's, a calculation, alongside the Titanic's famously inadequate compartmentalisation, that has shaped maritime safety regulation for over a century.

In our case, the detail that reframes this for me is that floating was never really about the material at all, it is a statement about the whole shape's average density compared to the surrounding water, which is exactly why the same steel that sinks as a block carries entire cargo ships across oceans once it is shaped hollow.
