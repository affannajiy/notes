## In one sentence

Nokia went from selling more mobile phones than the next four biggest manufacturers combined in 2007 to selling its entire phone business to Microsoft for about \$7.2 billion in 2013, because it kept improving hardware and its own Symbian software while Apple and Google rebuilt what a phone even was around apps and touchscreens.

## Situation

Nokia, a Finnish company originally in paper and rubber before moving into telecommunications, became the world's largest mobile phone maker by the late 1990s and held that position for well over a decade. In 2007, the year the iPhone launched, Nokia still held about 50% of the global mobile phone market and roughly 40% of the smartphone market specifically, run on its own Symbian operating system. By 2013, Nokia's smartphone market share had fallen to around 3%, and in September of that year Nokia sold its entire Devices and Services division, the phone business itself, to Microsoft.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Nokia's leadership treated smartphones as better hardware running smarter software written for that hardware, the same formula that had made it the market leader, while Apple and Google were redefining the smartphone as a platform for apps built by outside developers. Nokia had the manufacturing scale and the market share to have won either fight, but it kept fighting the first one for years after the second one had clearly started.

## What happened

Firstly, Symbian, Nokia's operating system, had been built and continuously modified since 1998 for older, simpler phones, and adapting it to compete with iOS's touch interface and app ecosystem proved slow and technically painful, since the underlying system was never designed around either of those ideas.

Secondly, a leaked 2011 memo from incoming CEO Stephen Elop, comparing Nokia's position to "standing on a burning platform", described a company aware of the danger but unable to move fast enough, partly because switching operating systems entirely meant abandoning years of Symbian development and the developers built around it.

Thirdly, Elop's chosen fix, in 2011, was to abandon Symbian and adopt Microsoft's Windows Phone instead of Google's Android, a smaller ecosystem than either of its two established rivals, which meant Nokia bet its recovery on the platform with the fewest existing apps and users of the three main options available to it.

Lastly, Windows Phone never gained enough market share or developer interest to reverse the decline, and by 2013 Microsoft judged it simpler to buy Nokia's phone unit outright than to keep relying on an independent partner losing money on every generation of device.

## Lessons

To begin with, having the resources and technical ability to compete is not the same as recognising which competition you are actually in. Nokia could have built a great touchscreen app-driven phone; it built a better version of the phone that was already being made obsolete instead.

In addition, a rescue bet made under pressure can fail for the same structural reason as the platform it replaced: fewer developers, fewer apps, fewer reasons to switch, since app ecosystems compound advantage for whoever already has the most users.

For me, I feel the "burning platform" memo is the most honest part of this whole story, a leader who correctly diagnosed the danger publicly and still could not move the company fast enough to escape it, which says something about how hard organisational change actually is compared to how easy it is to describe.
