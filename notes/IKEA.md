## In one sentence

IKEA's winding, forced-path store layout is not an accident of poor design, it is a deliberate strategy borrowed loosely from supermarket psychology, designed to maximise the time a shopper spends walking past product after product, since more exposure to more items reliably produces more items ending up in the cart than a straightforward, quick-exit layout ever would.

## What it does

IKEA designs, manufactures through outside suppliers and sells ready-to-assemble furniture and home goods through large warehouse-format stores worldwide, built around flat-pack packaging that keeps shipping costs low and lets customers transport furniture home themselves rather than relying on delivery.

## How it makes money

IKEA earns revenue primarily through direct furniture and home goods sales, but its low-price strategy depends heavily on flat-pack design and self-assembly shifting labour and shipping costs onto the customer, letting IKEA sell furniture at prices meaningfully lower than competitors who ship pre-assembled items and generally offer in-home delivery and setup as standard.

## Products

IKEA's furniture is deliberately named using a specific internal system, sofas and armchairs given Swedish place names, bookcases named after professions, a quirky naming convention that has itself become something of a cultural signature recognisable well beyond the products themselves. The company's in-store restaurant, serving Swedish dishes like meatballs at low prices, is often credited by retail analysts with meaningfully extending average visit length, keeping shoppers in the store, and near more products, for longer than they might otherwise stay.
