## In one sentence

A community of retail investors on Reddit's WallStreetBets forum noticed that professional hedge funds had bet so heavily against GameStop's stock that buying and holding it en masse could force those funds to buy the stock back at any price to close their losing bets, and doing exactly that in January 2021 pushed GameStop's share price up roughly 1,700% in under a month and forced at least one major hedge fund into an emergency bailout.

## Situation

GameStop, a struggling brick-and-mortar video game retailer, had become one of the most heavily "shorted" stocks on the US market by late 2020, meaning hedge funds and other investors had borrowed and sold huge numbers of GameStop shares betting the price would fall further, a bet that profits only if the stock keeps dropping. Retail investors on the WallStreetBets subreddit, particularly a user posting under the name "DeepFuckingValue" (Keith Gill), argued GameStop was undervalued and that the sheer scale of the short bets against it created a structural vulnerability. Starting in January 2021, a coordinated wave of retail buying pushed GameStop's share price from around \$17 to a peak of about \$483 by 28 January 2021, an increase of roughly 1,700% in under a month.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is what is called a short squeeze: when a stock that has been heavily shorted starts rising instead of falling, the funds holding those short positions face escalating losses the longer they wait, and eventually many are forced to buy back shares at whatever price is available just to close the losing position and stop the bleeding, and that forced buying itself pushes the price up even further, creating a feedback loop the original short sellers cannot easily escape.

## What happened

Firstly, Melvin Capital, one of the hedge funds most heavily shorting GameStop, lost an estimated 53% of its value in January 2021 alone and required a \$2.75 billion emergency capital injection from other investment firms just to stay solvent, a scale of loss that made the retail-driven squeeze a genuine Wall Street crisis rather than a curiosity.

Secondly, on 28 January 2021, Robinhood and several other retail trading apps restricted users from buying GameStop and a handful of similarly targeted stocks, allowing only selling, a decision that triggered immediate accusations of protecting hedge funds at retail investors' expense and led to a US congressional hearing in February 2021 where Robinhood's CEO testified about the restriction.

Thirdly, the episode drew serious regulatory attention to the mechanics of short selling, payment for order flow, the way trading apps like Robinhood earn money by routing trades through specific market makers, and the influence of coordinated retail activity on social media, with the SEC publishing a lengthy report on the event in October 2021 without ultimately finding clear evidence of illegal market manipulation by the retail traders involved.

## Lessons

To begin with, a sufficiently coordinated, large group of small individual investors can move a stock's price meaningfully when a structural vulnerability, extreme short interest here, exists to exploit, a possibility most of Wall Street had not seriously priced in before January 2021.

In addition, the trading platform restriction moment revealed how much practical power intermediary apps hold over retail access to markets during a crisis, regardless of the platform's stated neutrality the rest of the time, which is why the incident remains a reference point in debates about brokerage conflicts of interest.
