## In one sentence

Traffic lights use red, yellow and green mainly because railways had already standardised on those colours for signalling decades before cars existed, and the specific colour choices themselves trace back to red and green having the strongest wavelength contrast visible at a distance, with yellow later added as a distinct, unmistakable warning colour between the two.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "red means stop and green means go" feels so intuitive that it is easy to assume the colours were picked arbitrarily and simply became familiar through repetition, but the actual history runs the other way, railway signalling had already established the red-green convention long before the first traffic light appeared for road vehicles. Railways began using red as a stop signal as early as the 19th century, since red light has the longest wavelength among visible colours, meaning it scatters least in fog, rain or haze and remains visible at the greatest distance under poor conditions, a genuinely practical reason to reserve it for the signal a driver or train operator absolutely cannot afford to miss.

Green was chosen as the clear signal partly by process of elimination, early railway signalling actually used white for "go" and red for "stop," but white lights proved dangerously easy to confuse with other ordinary lights nearby, prompting a shift to green specifically because it was rare enough in the environment to stand out unambiguously as a dedicated signal colour. Yellow, added later as railways and then road traffic systems developed a need for a distinct "caution" signal between stop and go, was chosen partly because it sits at a wavelength clearly distinguishable from both red and green even for most forms of colour vision deficiency, reducing the risk of a driver misreading the warning signal as either extreme.

## Example / non-example

- Example: the first electric traffic signal, installed in Cleveland, Ohio in 1914, adopted red and green directly from existing railway signalling convention rather than inventing a new colour scheme from scratch, confirming the borrowing happened deliberately, not coincidentally.
- Non-example: some countries' pedestrian signals use blue rather than green for "walk," a genuine exception rooted in specific linguistic and cultural history in places like Japan, showing the red-yellow-green convention, while dominant, is not a universal law of nature, just an unusually well established and widely adopted standard.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a fun historical fact? Firstly, this is a clear example of a safety standard emerging from a genuinely practical constraint, visibility distance under bad conditions, rather than from arbitrary colour preference, and that same red-longest-wavelength logic still shows up today in tail lights, emergency vehicle lighting and hazard warning systems generally.

In addition, the traffic light's borrowing from rail signalling is a reminder that many "obvious" modern conventions were not designed fresh for their eventual use, they were inherited from an earlier system solving a closely related problem, which is a pattern that shows up again in this vault's coverage of QWERTY and keyboard shortcuts, both cases of an early standard's logic outliving and shaping a much later technology.

In our case, the detail that stands out most is that yellow's specific placement, positioned deliberately as an unmistakable middle signal rather than simply "half red, half green," reflects a real design decision about colour vision and cognitive clarity, not an afterthought bolted onto an already-working two-colour system.
