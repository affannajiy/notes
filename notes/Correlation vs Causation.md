## In one sentence

Correlation means two things tend to change together, while causation means one thing actually makes the other happen, and confusing the two is one of the most common statistical mistakes precisely because correlated data is easy to spot while proving causation usually requires a much more demanding kind of evidence, often a controlled experiment.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "correlation does not imply causation" gets repeated so often it can start to sound like a slogan rather than something worth actually understanding, so it helps to look at why correlated data alone genuinely cannot settle the question. When two variables move together, ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rising in summer being a classic example, there are at least three separate explanations that could produce that exact same correlated pattern, one variable could cause the other, the relationship could run backward, or, most commonly, both could be driven by some third factor neither variable directly causes, in this case, hot weather driving both more swimming and more ice cream buying.

Establishing genuine causation instead requires ruling out those alternative explanations, which is exactly what a well designed controlled experiment does, by randomly assigning subjects to different conditions, researchers can be confident any resulting difference stems from the variable being tested, since random assignment should, on average, spread every other possible factor evenly between groups. This in turn is why correlational studies, however large or statistically significant, cannot on their own establish causation the way a genuine randomised experiment can, no amount of observed co-occurrence rules out a hidden third cause sitting behind both variables.

## Example / non-example

- Example: countries with more television sets per household historically also had lower infant mortality, a real correlation, but not because television ownership itself reduces infant deaths, both are driven by a country's general wealth and healthcare infrastructure, a classic confounding variable sitting behind an otherwise real statistical relationship.
- Non-example: a randomised controlled trial giving one group a new medication and a placebo to another, then measuring outcomes, can support a genuine causal claim, since random assignment specifically rules out the confounding-variable explanation that plagues purely observational correlation.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a statistics classroom? Firstly, this distinction is exactly why medical and policy claims lean so heavily on randomised controlled trials specifically rather than simply observing existing patterns, since only genuine random assignment can rule out the hidden third-factor explanation that observational data alone cannot exclude.

In addition, media coverage of scientific studies frequently blurs this line, headlines describing an observational study's correlation using causal language, "X causes Y," when the underlying research only ever demonstrated a correlation, a distortion that matters because it can shape public understanding and even policy decisions based on evidence that does not actually support the causal claim being made.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is a simple habit, whenever encountering a correlated pair of facts, asking what third factor might be driving both before assuming either one causes the other, a cheap mental check that catches a surprising number of otherwise convincing-sounding claims.
