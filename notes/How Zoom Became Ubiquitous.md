## In one sentence

Zoom won the video call market not by inventing video calling, since Skype, WebEx and Google Hangouts already existed, but by being the one option reliable and simple enough that a link worked instantly in any browser without an account, which mattered enormously the moment hundreds of millions of people needed to video call overnight in March 2020.

## Situation

Zoom Video Communications was founded in 2011 by Eric Yuan, a former Cisco WebEx engineer who left specifically because he believed WebEx's video quality and reliability could be built better from scratch. The company grew steadily through the 2010s as a business tool, going public in April 2019. Then the COVID-19 pandemic hit: Zoom's daily meeting participants went from about 10 million in December 2019 to over 300 million by April 2020, a roughly 30-fold jump in four months, as schools, offices and even family gatherings moved onto video calls almost overnight.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that video conferencing had existed for years without becoming a default habit for most people, since older tools like Skype and WebEx were seen as clunky, required accounts or app installs, and often demanded technical troubleshooting that made spontaneous calling annoying rather than easy. Zoom's core bet, made years before the pandemic, was that removing every point of friction, one click on a link, no account required to join, working reliably on a weak home internet connection, would matter more than any feature list.

## What happened

Firstly, the "click the link, join instantly" design meant that during the pandemic a teacher, a company or even a grandmother organising a family call did not need to walk anyone through account creation, which is precisely the barrier that made competitors' tools feel harder in a moment when non-technical adoption exploded.

Secondly, Zoom's engineering had specifically prioritised working well over unreliable connections, a legacy of Yuan's frustration with WebEx's video quality, which meant Zoom degraded gracefully on the patchy home Wi-Fi millions of new users suddenly relied on, rather than freezing or dropping calls the way some rivals did under the same conditions.

Thirdly, "Zoombombing", where uninvited strangers joined and disrupted public meeting links, forced the company into rapid public fixes, adding waiting rooms and default passwords within weeks in April 2020, fast enough to keep trust from collapsing during the exact period it was gaining the most new users.

Lastly, "Zoom" itself became a verb, the clearest sign a brand has won a category, the way "Google it" replaced "search the web" years earlier, cementing the company's name as the generic term even among users who later switched to Microsoft Teams or Google Meet for other calls.

## Lessons

To begin with, being first or having the most features matters less than working without friction when a mass, non-technical audience suddenly needs the thing at once. Zoom had years of quiet engineering focused on reliability that only became visible once demand tested every competitor at once.

In addition, a crisis-driven security failure handled fast in public can be survived, and even reinforced trust, while the same failure ignored or downplayed would likely have ended the growth spike Zoom was riding.

For me, I feel the clearest lesson is that infrastructure decisions made years before anyone was watching, prioritising a bad connection over a good one, are exactly the kind of unglamorous engineering choice that decides who wins when the real test finally arrives.
