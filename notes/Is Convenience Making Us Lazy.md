## My take in one line

Convenience is not making us lazy, it is making us selective about which effort we still bother with, and the proof is that the same person who cannot cook rice without a rice cooker will still spend three hours on a hobby nobody is paying them for.

## Why this is interesting

Every generation gets accused of this by the one before it. My parents heard it about calculators, my grandparents heard it about washing machines, and now it is food delivery apps and voice assistants. Truth be told, the accusation never changes shape, only the gadget does, which makes me suspicious "convenience causes laziness" is less an observation and more a reflex.

## The case for

Firstly, the numbers on food delivery are real. Grab and foodpanda between them handle a large share of urban Malaysian meal orders, and a 2023 Ipsos survey found that over half of Malaysian delivery app users order at least once a week, a habit built entirely around not walking to the kopitiam downstairs. In addition, physical skills do appear to be fading: fewer young adults report knowing how to change a car tyre or read a paper map than a generation ago, according to AAA and National Geographic, and that drop is hard to explain without pointing at GPS and roadside assistance apps doing the job instead.

Furthermore, there is a psychological account for why this would happen. Behavioural economists call it the path of least resistance: given two ways to get the same result, people default to the easier one almost every time. Convenience products are built to lower that cost, so more convenience should mechanically produce less effort spent on that specific task.

## The case against

On the other hand, effort has not disappeared, it has moved. The same delivery generation runs marathons in record numbers, Malaysia's parkrun events have grown year on year since restarting after the pandemic, and home baking, gardening and DIY content are among the fastest growing categories on YouTube. Nobody forces a person to knead sourdough by hand when bread is RM3 at the shop, yet millions choose to.

Moreover, economists call this substitution, not laziness: convenience frees up time that went to the low-value task, whereby I would rather order nasi lemak than cook it, and get to the gym, my chosen effort, on time. Efficiency is not laziness, since laziness means avoiding effort altogether, and the data shows effort simply redirected toward what a person actually wants to be good at.

## Where I land

In my opinion, convenience is lazy-making only for the specific skill it replaces, and neutral or even effort-generating everywhere else. I have not changed a tyre in years because roadside assistance exists, and I will not pretend otherwise. But I also would not have had time to learn to code, cook a proper curry from scratch, or train for a 10K if I still spent an hour a day on tasks convenience now handles in five minutes.

(In our case, my mother still insists on hand-washing certain clothes despite owning a washing machine, so apparently convenience did not even fully win at home.)

## What would change my mind

A study tracking the same people's total weekly effort before and after adopting a wave of convenience tech would settle this properly. If total effort spent genuinely fell rather than shifted, that would be real laziness, and I have not seen that study done well yet.
