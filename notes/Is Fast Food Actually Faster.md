## My take in one line

Fast food is faster than cooking from scratch, but it is often not faster than the alternatives it actually competes with in practice, a bowl of instant noodles at home or a nearby kopitiam order, so "fast" describes the kitchen process behind the counter more than it describes the actual wait time the customer experiences.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I have sat in a drive-through line for fifteen minutes more than once, longer than frying an egg and making toast at home, so the name itself started to feel like a marketing claim rather than a guarantee, and I wanted to check whether that feeling holds up.

## The case for

Firstly, the fast food model is built around minimising kitchen time: standardised recipes, pre-prepped ingredients and assembly-line cooking get food from order to tray in a couple of minutes, dramatically faster than home cooking or a restaurant preparing a dish from raw ingredients on request. In addition, drive-through timing studies, including annual benchmarking from QSR Magazine, show top chains averaging under three minutes from order to window during normal hours, genuinely hard to beat for a hot cooked meal.

Furthermore, fast food solves a different problem than home cooking does entirely, availability without preparation, no shopping, no cleanup, no waiting for ingredients to be on hand, which is a form of speed that has nothing to do with the literal minutes ticking by and everything to do with removing steps a home-cooked meal would otherwise require before cooking even starts.

## The case against

On the other hand, that same QSR Magazine benchmarking data shows average drive-through times climbing over the past decade in the US, from under 3 minutes in the early 2010s to over 4 minutes by the early 2020s at several major chains, driven by menu complexity and higher order volume, meaning the fastest part of "fast food" has itself been getting measurably slower even as the category keeps the name.

Moreover, once queueing, driving and any wait inside are counted, fast food frequently loses to genuinely fast alternatives already sitting in a kitchen: instant noodles in three minutes, microwaved leftovers, or a nearby hawker stall with a two-minute wait, none needing a car or a queue at all. So where does "fast food is fast" break down? Precisely at peak hours, lunch and dinner rushes, exactly when most people order it because they are, ironically, most short on time.

## Where I land

In my opinion, fast food earns its name only against cooking a full meal from raw ingredients, and loses that claim against the other quick options most time-pressed people actually have. In our case, driving to a fast food outlet during a rushed weekday lunch has cost me more total time, door to door, than making a sandwich at home would have, often enough that I now only choose it when I want the food itself, not when I am optimising for speed.

(To which a friend, defending his drive-through loyalty during a long queue, said, "at least I am not the one cooking," which is a genuinely different argument than speed, and probably the more honest one.)

## What would change my mind

A direct, controlled comparison measuring door-to-food time for fast food against realistic at-home quick meals, across a representative sample of actual weekday rush hours rather than off-peak testing, showing fast food reliably winning, would change this take.
