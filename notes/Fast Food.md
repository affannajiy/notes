## In one sentence

Fast food's defining innovation was never really the food itself, it was applying the factory assembly line's core idea, breaking a complex process into small, repeatable, standardised steps, to something as personal and variable as cooking, which is exactly what let a burger taste nearly identical whether bought in one city or another entirely.

## What it is

Fast food refers to food prepared and served quickly, typically through a standardised menu, streamlined kitchen process and often a drive-through or counter-service format, designed to minimise both preparation time and the variation between individual locations of the same chain.

## Types

Burger chains, McDonald's and Burger King among the most globally recognised, built the format's original template, a small fixed menu, assembly-line kitchen process and heavy reliance on car-friendly drive-through service. Fried chicken chains, KFC prominent among them, adapted the same standardised model to a different core product, proving the fast food format could transfer across cuisines rather than being tied to the burger specifically. Regional and local fast food formats, Malaysia's own network of mamak stalls and kopitiam outlets serving quick, affordable meals, achieve broadly similar speed and consistency goals through a genuinely different, less industrialised operating model built around a smaller, family-run scale rather than corporate standardisation.

## Terms

A few terms recur across fast food discussion worth defining plainly. "Quick service restaurant," often abbreviated QSR, is the industry's own formal term for the fast food format, used in business and financial reporting rather than everyday conversation. "Franchise," a business model where a company licenses its brand, recipes and operating system to independent local operators in exchange for fees and a share of revenue, is the structure behind nearly every major fast food chain's rapid global expansion, letting a company grow without funding every new location's construction itself.

## History in moments

White Castle, founded in the United States in 1921, is often credited as the first true fast food chain, introducing standardised recipes and a small, focused menu years before McDonald's existed. McDonald's own 1948 "Speedee Service System," reorganising its kitchen around assembly-line principles borrowed directly from automobile manufacturing, became the template the entire industry later copied and refined. The global expansion of American fast food chains through the late 20th century, McDonald's opening in Moscow in 1990 being a particularly symbolic milestone, was often read at the time as a marker of a country's economic opening to Western consumer culture.

## Malaysian connection

Malaysia's fast food market blends major international chains with strong local competitors, Marrybrown notably building a genuinely successful home-grown fried chicken chain that has expanded into several other Southeast Asian and Middle Eastern markets, proving the standardised fast food model works for local operators too, not only multinational brands.

## What's happening now

Fast food chains are increasingly investing in delivery-only "ghost kitchens," locations with no dine-in space at all built purely to fulfil app orders, a shift accelerated by the broader rise of food delivery apps already covered in this vault's [[The Rise of Food Delivery]] note.
