## In one sentence

Those stiff bristle brushes running along the edges of an escalator's steps exist specifically as a safety measure, a physical reminder and barrier discouraging feet, loose shoelaces and clothing from drifting too close to the genuinely dangerous gap where a moving step disappears beneath the stationary side panel.

## Explain it

Truth be told, an escalator step needs a small functional gap where it meets the fixed side skirt panel, since the step itself is constantly moving while the panel stays still, and that gap is precisely where serious injuries have historically occurred, a shoe, sandal strap or trailing piece of clothing pulled into the mechanism as the moving step slides underneath the stationary panel. The bristle brushes, introduced widely from the 1990s onward following a number of documented injury incidents, serve two overlapping purposes at once, physically nudging a foot or object away from that dangerous edge before it drifts too close, and giving a rider a visible, tactile cue marking exactly where the safe standing area actually ends.

This in turn is why the brushes are positioned specifically along the step's outer edges rather than anywhere else on the escalator, that precise location is exactly where the moving step transitions past the fixed skirt panel, the single point along the entire mechanism where the dangerous gap actually exists. In addition, escalator manufacturers eventually made these brushes close to a genuine industry standard specifically after safety regulators in several countries, particularly following incidents involving young children's shoes, began treating their inclusion as an expected safety feature on newly installed and refurbished escalators.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a young child wearing soft, flexible sandals standing too close to an escalator's side panel benefits directly from the brush's gentle physical nudge keeping their footwear away from the dangerous gap edge, exactly the situation the design change was intended to address.
- Non-example: an elevator, despite serving a broadly similar vertical transport purpose, has no equivalent moving step mechanism at all, and therefore no comparable gap-related injury risk, which is exactly why elevators have never needed this specific safety feature.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a curious detail noticed while riding an escalator? Firstly, the brush's introduction is a genuinely useful example of safety design responding directly to documented real-world injury data, retrofitted onto an already widely deployed piece of infrastructure once the specific injury pattern became clear enough to act on.

In addition, this pattern of adding a low-cost physical safety feature in response to a specific, recurring injury type recurs across many other public infrastructure designs, rounded edges, warning strips and tactile paving among them, each addressing one particular, well documented hazard rather than attempting to solve safety in some more abstract, general sense.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a small design feature easy to overlook entirely often carries a specific, documented safety history behind it, a pattern worth remembering the next time an everyday object's seemingly minor design choice seems unexplained at first glance.
