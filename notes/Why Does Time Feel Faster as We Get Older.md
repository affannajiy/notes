## In one sentence

Time feels like it speeds up with age mainly because novelty, not clock time, is what the brain uses to judge how much time has passed in retrospect, and adult life simply contains far fewer genuinely new experiences per year than childhood does, leaving fewer distinct memories for the brain to measure a year's length against afterward.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "time flies as you get older" is one of those sayings almost everyone agrees with instinctively, but the mechanism behind it turns out to be about memory density, not about clocks running differently. The brain does not perceive the passage of time directly the way it perceives sound or light, instead, retrospective time judgement, how long a period felt looking back on it, is built substantially from how many distinct, memorable events that period contained. A childhood summer packed with new places, new skills and new social situations creates many more distinct memories than an adult year following a familiar daily routine, and more memories packed into the same objective duration makes that duration feel, in retrospect, longer.

This in turn is why the proportional theory offers a second, complementary explanation, a year is a much larger fraction of a five-year-old's entire lived experience than it is of a fifty-year-old's, and that changing ratio may shape the felt weight of a year even independent of memory density. Together these two effects mean adult life, with its greater routine and its shrinking proportional share of total lived experience, produces both fewer standout memories and a smaller relative unit, both pushing the same direction, toward years feeling like they pass faster.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a two-week trip somewhere entirely new, full of unfamiliar food, routes and decisions, often feels, in retrospect, far longer than two ordinary weeks at home, consistent with novelty and memory density driving retrospective time judgement rather than the actual clock duration.
- Non-example: two weeks spent following an identical daily schedule, waking at the same time, doing the same commute, tends to compress into a single blurred memory afterward, despite covering the exact same number of days as the trip, showing routine specifically suppresses the retrospective sense of duration.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how people might think about their own time in practice? Firstly, this is exactly why deliberately introducing novelty into an otherwise routine adult life, a new hobby, an unfamiliar route to work, a new skill, is sometimes suggested as a way to counter the "time speeding up" feeling, since it directly targets the mechanism, memory density, behind the effect rather than trying to fight the sensation itself.

In addition, this explains why people frequently report their most vividly remembered years being ones containing a major life change, moving cities, starting a new job, having a child, since these periods force exactly the kind of sustained novelty that produces dense, retrospectively long-feeling stretches of memory.

In our case, the detail that reframes this for me is that "time feels faster" is not really about time at all, it is about how much a period gave the brain worth remembering, which means the feeling is, in principle, something a person has more influence over than the phrase "time flies" usually implies.
