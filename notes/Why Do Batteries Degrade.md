## In one sentence

Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries degrade because the repeated chemical reactions of charging and discharging gradually and irreversibly damage the internal electrode materials that store the battery's charge, meaning capacity loss is not a fault or defect, it is the unavoidable, cumulative result of the exact same chemistry that lets the battery work at all.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "batteries just wear out" is true but skips the more interesting detail, which is what "wearing out" actually consists of at a chemical level. A lithium-ion battery stores energy by moving lithium ions back and forth between two electrodes through a liquid electrolyte, and every single charge and discharge cycle causes small, mostly permanent side reactions alongside the main, intended reaction, gradually consuming usable lithium and slightly restructuring the electrode material each time.

The most significant of these side effects is the growth of a layer called the solid electrolyte interphase on the negative electrode, which forms as a byproduct of normal operation and, while partly protective, thickens with every cycle in a way that consumes lithium and increases internal resistance, both of which reduce how much charge the battery can ultimately hold or deliver quickly. This in turn is why battery degradation accelerates under heat and at very high or very low states of charge, all three conditions speed up these same underlying side reactions, which is exactly why manufacturers recommend avoiding leaving a phone at 100 percent charge in a hot car, that specific combination stresses the chemistry in the way most likely to accelerate long-term capacity loss.

## Example / non-example

- Example: an electric vehicle battery kept mostly between 20 and 80 percent charge, and avoided extreme heat, typically shows meaningfully slower capacity decline over years than an identical battery repeatedly charged to 100 percent and left in hot conditions, consistent with the side reactions being temperature and charge-level dependent rather than purely a function of total cycles alone.
- Non-example: a battery that has simply been sitting completely unused in storage still degrades slowly over time, called calendar ageing, distinct from cycle-based degradation, since some of the same chemical side reactions continue at a slow rate even without active charging or discharging.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how people can extend battery life in practice? Firstly, this is exactly why modern phones and electric vehicles increasingly include software-based charge limiting features, capping charging at a lower percentage or slowing charging speed near full capacity specifically to reduce the time a battery spends at the high-charge, high-stress state that accelerates degradation.

In addition, this is why fast charging, while convenient, does contribute somewhat to faster long-term degradation compared to slower charging, the higher currents involved increase heat and stress on the electrode materials during each charging session, a real trade-off between short-term convenience and long-term battery health that manufacturers increasingly try to manage through smarter charging algorithms rather than leaving entirely to the user.

In our case, the detail that reframes this for me is that battery degradation is not really a flaw being tolerated, it is the same core electrochemical process that makes the battery rechargeable at all, running slightly imperfectly every single time, which is why no lithium-ion battery, however well designed, fully escapes it.
