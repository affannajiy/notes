## In one sentence

Streaming was widely predicted to make cinemas obsolete the way it hollowed out video rental and severely disrupted physical media, but cinemas survived by leaning into exactly the two things streaming structurally cannot replicate at home, a large shared screen and sound experience, and a genuinely social outing, rather than by competing with streaming's convenience directly.

## Situation

Through the 2010s, as streaming grew and pulled significant viewing away from traditional television and physical media, industry commentary regularly predicted cinemas would follow the same declining path as video rental stores and DVD sales had. The core problem at hand for cinemas was real, streaming did capture a large share of casual, at-home viewing that once might have been a cinema visit, and the COVID-19 pandemic then forced a genuinely severe, sudden test of that pressure by closing cinemas outright for extended periods across most of the world.

## Core problem

The one tension this case turns on is that cinemas and streaming were never actually offering the same product despite competing for some of the same entertainment budget, streaming sells convenience and low cost per viewing, while cinemas, once forced to justify their existence against that convenience, had to lean fully into the parts of their experience convenience genuinely cannot match, scale of screen and sound, and a shared, out-of-the-house social occasion.

## What happened

Firstly, cinema attendance did fall meaningfully through the 2010s even before the pandemic, confirming streaming had genuinely captured some previously cinema-bound viewing, particularly for smaller, lower-budget films less suited to a big-screen experience in the first place.

Secondly, the pandemic forced the most severe possible test of cinema's necessity, with most cinemas closed for extended stretches through 2020 and into 2021, and several studios experimented directly with releasing major films on streaming instead, a real, live trial of the "cinemas are optional" hypothesis.

Thirdly, once cinemas reopened, attendance recovered strongest specifically for large-scale blockbusters, films built around visual spectacle and event-driven communal viewing, while mid-budget dramas and smaller films shifted more permanently toward streaming and home viewing, splitting the market by film type rather than eliminating cinema outright.

Lastly, cinema chains responded by investing further into premium formats, IMAX, higher-end seating, better sound systems, deliberately widening the experiential gap between a cinema visit and watching the same film at home, rather than competing with streaming on price or convenience where cinema could never realistically win.

## Lessons

To begin with, a technology does not need to be strictly better across every dimension to threaten an incumbent, streaming only needed to be better on convenience and cost to capture the viewing occasions where those two factors mattered most, leaving cinema to survive specifically in the occasions where they mattered least.

In addition, an industry facing disruption survives better by doubling down on its genuinely unique, hard-to-replicate strengths than by trying to match a disruptor's advantage directly, cinema's bet on scale and spectacle rather than convenience is a clear example of exactly that kind of repositioning.
