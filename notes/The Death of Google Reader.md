## In one sentence

Google Reader was the most popular RSS feed reader in the world, a tool that let users follow updates from any website in one place without relying on a social network's algorithm to decide what they saw, and Google shut it down in 2013 as part of a broader push toward Google+, a decision now widely seen as one of the clearest examples of a genuinely useful product killed for strategic reasons rather than because it failed.

## Situation

Google launched Google Reader in 2005 as a tool for reading RSS feeds, a format, short for Really Simple Syndication, that lets websites publish a machine-readable list of their latest posts so a reader app can pull updates from many sites into one feed, without any social platform or algorithm deciding what gets shown. By the early 2010s Google Reader had become the dominant RSS reader, used by millions, including journalists and a large technically engaged community who relied on it as their primary way of tracking news and blogs. On 13 March 2013, Google announced it would shut down on 1 July 2013, citing declining usage, a claim many former users disputed given the product's continued heavy use within specific communities.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Google Reader, while genuinely useful to a loyal user base, did not fit Google's strategic direction at the time, which was pushing hard toward Google+ and a more social, engagement-driven model of information discovery, and RSS readers by nature let users curate their own information sources directly, bypassing any algorithm or social feed entirely, a model that generated no advertising or engagement data the way Google's other priorities at the time did.

## What happened

Firstly, Google's head of social products at the time, reported in contemporary tech press coverage, had pushed for Reader's social sharing features to be removed in favour of directing that activity toward Google+ instead, a 2011 change that frustrated Reader's community and cut some of the product's most valued functionality before the full shutdown.

Secondly, the backlash was large enough that it became one of the most visible examples of the "Google Graveyard," a term already used informally for Google's habit of launching and abandoning products, and Reader's closure became the case most frequently cited afterward.

Thirdly, competitors and former Reader engineers built replacement RSS readers, including Feedly, which absorbed a large share of Reader's displaced user base within weeks of the shutdown announcement, proving there was still real, sustained demand for exactly the kind of product Google had just decided to abandon.

Lastly, Google+ itself, the priority Reader was arguably sacrificed for, failed within a few years and shut down by 2019, making the tradeoff look worse over time, since demand for the killed product outlived the pivot it was sacrificed for.

## Lessons

To begin with, a product being retired "due to low usage" is not always the full story, since a company's internal strategic priorities can make even a well used, well loved product expendable if it does not serve whatever the company is betting on next.

In addition, the durability of Feedly and other RSS readers built specifically to absorb Reader's displaced users shows that killing a product does not kill the underlying demand for it, and other companies are often willing to step into exactly the gap a larger company leaves behind.
