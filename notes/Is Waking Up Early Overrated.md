## My take in one line

Waking up early is not virtuous, it is just convenient for a world built around office hours, and the actual science of chronotypes, meaning a person's natural sleep-wake tendency, shows plenty of people are genuinely wired to peak later in the day and pay a real cost forcing themselves into a 5am routine that was never designed around their biology.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, "successful people wake up at 5am" is one of the most repeated productivity claims online, usually backed by a handful of CEOs rather than any actual study of the wider population, and I wanted to know whether the claim survives contact with sleep research or whether it is mostly survivorship bias dressed up as a life lesson.

## The case for

Firstly, waking early genuinely does align with how most workplaces, schools and social schedules run, so a person who wakes early gets quiet, uninterrupted hours before the world starts demanding their attention, which is a real practical advantage regardless of biology. In addition, some studies, including research referenced by the Journal of Applied Social Psychology, found self-reported "morning people" tend to report higher proactivity and slightly better academic performance, though these studies mostly measure people already living in a system built for morning schedules, which muddies how much is biology versus fit with the environment.

Furthermore, morning light exposure is genuinely linked to better regulated circadian rhythms, the internal clock that governs sleep and hormone timing, so an early riser who gets outside shortly after waking does get a real benefit independent of any productivity culture claim.

## The case against

On the other hand, chronotype research is fairly clear that wake preference is substantially biological, not just a habit anyone can adopt through willpower. A well known analysis by chronobiologist Till Roenneberg found chronotype distributions vary widely across a population and shift predictably with age, whereby teenagers and young adults skew later almost universally, which is a biological pattern, not a discipline failure, and forcing early schedules onto this group correlates with worse academic performance and mood in multiple studies.

Moreover, "social jetlag", researchers' term for the mismatch between a person's body clock and their forced schedule, has been linked in several studies to worse metabolic health and mood outcomes, which suggests misaligned wake times carry a real physiological cost for a genuine night-type person forced into an early one's schedule.

## Where I land

In my opinion, waking up early suits people whose biology already leans that way, and costs the rest a performance dressed up as universal virtue by people who happen to be the former. In our case, my most productive university periods were never the 5am ones I forced through a phase of productivity content, they were the ones where I matched study blocks to when I actually felt sharp, mid-morning through early afternoon, not dawn.

(To which a very productive 5am-rising friend once said, when I explained this, "sounds like an excuse to sleep in," and I have decided that is a chronotype problem on his end, not an argument.)

## What would change my mind

A study showing forced early wake times improve measured output even for confirmed evening chronotypes, not just self-reported morning people already living in a system built for them, would meaningfully challenge this take.
