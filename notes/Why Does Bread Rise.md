## In one sentence

Bread rises because yeast, a living single-celled fungus mixed into the dough, feeds on sugars in the flour and produces carbon dioxide gas as a byproduct, and the dough's gluten network traps that gas in thousands of tiny pockets rather than letting it escape, which is what gives risen bread its light, airy structure.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "yeast makes bread rise" is the sentence everyone learns early, but the more interesting part is why the gas actually stays trapped instead of just bubbling straight out of a wet dough the way it would out of a glass of water. The answer is gluten, a stretchy protein network formed when two proteins naturally present in wheat flour, glutenin and gliadin, combine with water and get developed through kneading or mixing. Kneading is not just tradition, it physically stretches and aligns those gluten strands into a mesh strong and elastic enough to stretch around expanding gas bubbles without tearing, similar in function to a balloon's skin holding air inside rather than letting it diffuse straight through.

As yeast consumes the natural sugars in flour, it produces carbon dioxide and a small amount of alcohol as byproducts, a process called fermentation, and because that gluten mesh surrounds the yeast throughout the dough, the carbon dioxide it produces gets caught locally rather than pooling out to one spot, which is what creates the fine, even network of small air pockets running through a well risen loaf's crumb.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a wheat bread dough left to rise for an hour visibly doubles in size, since the yeast has had time to ferment sugars and produce enough trapped gas to expand the dough's gluten network substantially.
- Non-example: a gluten-free flour blend with no added gum or binder produces a dense, flat result even with the same yeast and rise time, since without gluten's elastic protein structure there is nothing to trap the carbon dioxide the yeast still produces, and the gas simply escapes.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how bread gets made in practice? Firstly, this is why recipes specify a warm rather than cold environment for rising, yeast is a living organism whose fermentation rate depends heavily on temperature, working fastest in the mid-20s to low-30s Celsius range and slowing drastically in the cold, which is also why refrigerating dough overnight, a technique called cold retarding, is used deliberately to slow fermentation for flavour development rather than speed.

In addition, overproofing, letting dough rise for too long, eventually weakens the gluten structure past its elastic limit, causing the trapped gas pockets to collapse rather than hold their shape once baked, which is why a dough left to rise far too long can produce a flatter, denser loaf than one risen for the correct, shorter time, counterintuitive as that sounds.

In our case, the detail that changed how I think about baking is that kneading is not really "working the dough" in some vague sense, it is specifically building the physical structure, the gluten mesh, that the entire rise depends on, meaning under-kneaded dough can fail to rise well even with plenty of active yeast and warmth.
