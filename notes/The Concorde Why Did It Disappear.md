## In one sentence

Concorde flew paying passengers across the Atlantic at twice the speed of sound for 27 years, and it was retired in 2003 not primarily because of the one fatal crash in 2000 but because it had never actually made commercial sense, burning roughly four times the fuel per passenger of an ordinary jet while carrying under a hundred people, a cost structure a single crash and a post-9/11 travel slump finally made impossible to keep absorbing.

## Situation

Concorde was developed jointly by the British and French governments starting in 1962 as the world's first and, so far, only commercially successful supersonic passenger airliner, entering service in 1976 and flying London to New York in around three and a half hours, roughly half an ordinary subsonic flight. Only 20 Concordes were ever built, flown by just British Airways and Air France, both heavily subsidised by their governments since ticket revenue never fully covered the enormous development and operating costs. On 25 July 2000, Air France Flight 4590 crashed shortly after takeoff from Paris, killing all 109 aboard and 4 on the ground, grounding the fleet for over a year. Concorde returned in November 2001 with safety fixes, but both airlines retired it permanently by 2003.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Concorde was a genuine technical triumph that never solved the basic economics of supersonic flight: it seated only 92 to 128 passengers, far fewer than a wide-body subsonic jet, while consuming far more fuel per passenger, so ticket prices had to sit well above ordinary business class fares just to approach covering costs, restricting the market to wealthy or expense-account travellers even before any incident hit demand.

## What happened

Firstly, the 2000 crash, caused by runway debris puncturing a tyre and rupturing a fuel tank, was the first and only fatal Concorde accident, but it eliminated Concorde's most valuable asset, an unblemished safety record, which had been central to convincing wealthy, safety-conscious travellers to pay the enormous premium at all.

Secondly, the September 2001 terrorist attacks in the US caused a sharp, industry-wide collapse in premium international air travel almost immediately after Concorde returned to service post-crash, hitting exactly the wealthy transatlantic business travel segment Concorde depended on most heavily for demand.

Thirdly, maintenance costs had been climbing for years as the ageing fleet's specialised parts grew harder and more expensive to source, since only 20 aircraft were ever built and no successor programme existed to share spare parts costs across, a problem that worsens yearly for any type with such a small production run.

Lastly, both airlines announced retirement in 2003, citing falling post-crash, post-9/11 demand and rising maintenance costs, unjustifiable even with the government backing that had propped up the programme for decades.

## Lessons

To begin with, a technically remarkable achievement can still fail commercially if its underlying economics, cost per passenger here, were never solved, only subsidised, and Concorde's story is now the standard reference point whenever a new supersonic aircraft venture is proposed and has to explain why it will not repeat the same failure.

In addition, a product whose value proposition rests heavily on reputation, an unblemished safety record for Concorde, is more fragile to a single incident than one competing mainly on price or convenience, since reputation-dependent premium products lose the exact thing justifying their premium the moment that reputation cracks.
