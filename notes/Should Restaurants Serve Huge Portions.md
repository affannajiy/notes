## My take in one line

Huge portions solve a value perception problem, not a hunger problem, since customers judge whether a price was fair mostly by how much food arrives on the plate, so restaurants keep serving more than most people can comfortably eat because the alternative, a smaller plate at the same price, reads as a worse deal even when the food itself is identical in quality.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I have left more restaurant meals half-finished than I have finished completely, and I used to feel vaguely guilty about that until I noticed it happens almost every time regardless of which restaurant, which suggests the portion problem is systemic rather than something specific to my own appetite.

## The case for

Firstly, generous portions do genuinely signal value in a way customers respond to immediately and viscerally, before they have even tasted the food, and this matters commercially since a customer's sense of getting a fair deal drives repeat visits and word of mouth as much as taste does. In addition, restaurants operating on thin margins in competitive markets often cannot easily raise prices without losing customers to a competitor, so portion size becomes the lever they can pull instead, offering more food at the same price rather than the same food at a lower one.

Furthermore, some cuisines genuinely expect abundance as part of hospitality itself, a host who serves too little is seen as stingy in several Malaysian traditions around feeding guests, so "huge portions" in some contexts reflects a value system, not a marketing trick.

## The case against

On the other hand, US researchers, including the Rudd Center for Food Policy, have documented that American restaurant portion sizes have grown substantially since the 1980s, in some categories more than doubling, a trend tracking closely with rising average calorie intake and obesity rates, though causation is harder to prove than correlation here.

Moreover, behavioural research on portion size, including studies by Brian Wansink on "unit bias," found people tend to eat close to whatever amount is put in front of them regardless of actual hunger, meaning larger portions measurably increase consumption in one sitting, undercutting the idea that a bigger plate is a value-neutral bonus for anyone who does not want to finish it.

## Where I land

In my opinion, huge portions are a reasonable business response to how customers judge value, but a genuinely costly default for anyone trying to eat according to actual hunger rather than what a plate happens to contain. In our case, I have started asking for a smaller portion or a to-go box before finishing at restaurants that clearly overserve, which feels slightly awkward every time but has noticeably improved how I feel after a meal compared to eating everything simply because it was already served.

(To which a waiter once replied, boxing my unfinished half of a pasta dish, "at least you are honest about it," which suggests I am not the only customer quietly negotiating with a portion designed for someone hungrier than me.)

## What would change my mind

Evidence that smaller, appropriately sized portions at a proportionally lower price genuinely retained customer satisfaction and repeat business at the same rate as oversized portions, in a real market test rather than a lab survey, would make me less confident portion size is purely about perceived value rather than a genuine feature people want.
