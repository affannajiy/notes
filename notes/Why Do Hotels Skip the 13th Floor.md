## In one sentence

Many hotels, particularly in the United States, skip labelling a 13th floor purely to accommodate widespread triskaidekaphobia, fear of the number 13, since even guests who do not personally believe in any superstition often still feel a vague unease about staying on a floor marked with that number, a consumer psychology concern real enough that renumbering the floor costs a hotel nothing while avoiding any possible negative impression.

## Explain it

Truth be told, the physical 13th floor still exists in nearly every one of these buildings, elevators simply skip straight from 12 to 14 in the displayed floor numbering, meaning the floor that would otherwise be labelled 13 gets called 14 instead, purely a labelling convention change rather than any actual missing physical floor. Fear or unease around the number 13 has deep, tangled cultural roots, some tracing it to the Last Supper's thirteen attendees in Christian tradition, others to older Norse mythology, and the superstition became especially entrenched in Western culture through the 19th and early 20th centuries.

This in turn is why hotel and office building developers, operating in a genuinely competitive market where even a small proportion of guests feeling uneasy could measurably affect bookings or perceived value, found renumbering floors to be an extremely low-cost way to sidestep the issue entirely, since the change requires nothing more than adjusting elevator button labels and floor signage rather than any actual structural modification. In addition, surveys conducted by American real estate and hospitality researchers have repeatedly found that a meaningful share of guests report some discomfort with the number, enough that many building developers consider the renumbering a straightforwardly sensible business decision rather than mere superstition-indulgence.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a guest booking a room on the "14th floor" of many American hotels is, often without realising it, actually staying on what is physically the building's thirteenth storey above ground level, the floor simply relabelled to sidestep any potential unease.
- Non-example: hotels in several other cultural contexts face a genuinely different numbering superstition entirely, many East Asian buildings skip the number 4 instead, since it sounds similar to the word for "death" in Mandarin, Cantonese and Japanese, showing that the specific number avoided varies by which culture's particular superstition a building's market is actually catering to.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a quirky building numbering fact? Firstly, this practice is a genuinely useful example of businesses accommodating widespread but scientifically baseless belief purely for commercial reasons, treating customer perception as worth managing regardless of whether the underlying fear has any rational basis at all.

In addition, this same logic extends into other design and marketing decisions across different cultures, product pricing, packaging quantities and even flight numbers sometimes deliberately avoiding culturally unlucky numbers specifically to avoid any needless negative association in a competitive market where perception genuinely shapes purchasing decisions.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is that a missing 13th floor reveals more about consumer psychology and cultural history than about the number itself, a reminder that widely shared belief, even when objectively irrational, can still shape real, costly business decisions.
