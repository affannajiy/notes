## My take in one line

More choice helps up to a point, giving people a real sense of control, then past that point it starts costing more in decision fatigue and regret than it adds in freedom, which is why a shop with 6 jam flavours reliably sells more jam than the same shop with 24.

## Why this is interesting

Truth be told, I used to assume more options were simply better by default, since choice sounds like freedom and freedom sounds unambiguously good. Then I spent twenty minutes once picking a shampoo out of forty nearly identical bottles and left with nothing, which is the exact experience psychologists built a whole research area around.

## The case for

Firstly, having no choice at all is genuinely worse for wellbeing, well established across decades of psychology research on autonomy and control, so some choice is clearly necessary, not optional, for people to feel any agency over their own lives. In addition, more choice does help in situations where the options are meaningfully different and a person has clear preferences, whereby a wider range of degree programmes or career paths lets someone find a genuinely better fit than a narrow set would, as opposed to a case where the options barely differ from each other.

Furthermore, some researchers, including Barry Schwartz himself in later work, note that people with a "maximiser" mindset, always seeking the objectively best option, suffer from choice overload far more than "satisficers," people content with a good-enough option, which suggests the problem is partly about mindset and only partly about the raw number of choices on offer.

## The case against

On the other hand, the most famous demonstration here is Iyengar and Lepper's 2000 "jam study," where a supermarket display offering 24 jam flavours attracted more browsers but only converted 3% of them into buyers, while a display offering just 6 flavours converted 30%, ten times more, despite drawing a smaller crowd. Fewer options, more actual purchases.

Moreover, Barry Schwartz's 2004 book The Paradox of Choice argues that excessive choice increases both decision-making effort beforehand and regret afterward, since more options mean more imagined alternatives to compare a decision against once it is made, whereby a person who picked one of six restaurants rarely agonises over the other five the way someone who picked from thirty restaurants often does.

## Where I land

In my opinion, the relationship between choice and happiness is not a straight line upward, it rises with the first handful of meaningfully different options and then flattens or drops once options multiply past what a person can actually evaluate without exhausting themselves. In our case, I now deliberately limit myself to comparing three or four options for anything low-stakes, groceries, a streaming show, a cheap purchase, and save the fuller research effort for decisions that genuinely deserve it, university choices or job offers, which has cut a surprising amount of daily decision fatigue.

(To which a friend replied, watching me refuse to scroll further on a food delivery app, "you have decided decision paralysis is a personality trait now," which is fair, and also the point.)

## What would change my mind

A large-scale replication showing the jam study's effect reverses or vanishes for higher-stakes decisions specifically, rather than just everyday low-stakes ones, would push me to treat "more choice, more happiness" as broadly true rather than context-dependent.
