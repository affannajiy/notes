## In one sentence

App-based food delivery grew from a niche convenience into a major part of how many people eat by combining three things that had to mature together, smartphones with GPS, a large flexible gig workforce willing to deliver on demand, and restaurants willing to give up a meaningful commission in exchange for reaching customers they could not otherwise serve.

## Situation

Restaurant delivery existed long before apps, pizza chains built entire business models around it decades earlier, but it was limited to whichever restaurants ran their own delivery staff and vehicles, generally larger chains with the volume to justify the cost. The core problem at hand for any company hoping to build a general-purpose delivery platform was that most restaurants, especially smaller independent ones, could never justify hiring, training and scheduling their own delivery staff just to serve a fraction of their customers who wanted food brought to them.

## Core problem

The one tension delivery platforms had to solve was matching unpredictable, spiky demand, meal times cluster heavily around lunch and dinner, with a delivery workforce large enough to cover peak demand without needing to pay that same workforce during the quiet hours between meals, a problem the gig economy's on-demand, no-fixed-shift labour model was specifically well suited to solve.

## What happened

Firstly, smartphone GPS and mapping matured enough by the early 2010s to make real-time order tracking and efficient route assignment for a fleet of independent drivers technically workable, a precondition without which none of the later business model made sense.

Secondly, platforms like Grab, covered separately in this vault, DoorDash, Uber Eats and Foodpanda built two-sided marketplaces connecting restaurants, independent delivery drivers and customers, charging restaurants a commission, often 15 to 30 percent of an order's value, in exchange for order volume and delivery logistics the restaurant did not have to build itself.

Thirdly, the COVID-19 pandemic drove an enormous, sudden surge in food delivery demand as dine-in restaurants closed or restricted capacity through 2020 and 2021, permanently shifting many customers' habits even after restaurants reopened fully.

Lastly, the underlying economics have proven difficult for platforms themselves even as usage grew, since most food delivery companies operated at a loss for years, subsidising both restaurant commissions and driver pay with investor funding while competing hard on price to win market share, a pattern only recently shifting toward profitability at some platforms as competition consolidated.

## Lessons

To begin with, a platform solving a genuine convenience problem for customers and restaurants can still struggle to turn that usage into profit, since intense competition between several well-funded platforms chasing the same market pushed commissions and delivery fees below what covered the actual cost of the service for years.

In addition, the gig workforce model that made rapid scaling possible has drawn sustained scrutiny over driver pay and labour protections, an ongoing legal and political fight in multiple countries that shows a business model's technical elegance does not settle the separate question of whether it treats the people running it fairly.
