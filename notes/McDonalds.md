## In one sentence

McDonald's became a global system rather than just a restaurant chain because Ray Kroc, the man who franchised it, understood something the founding McDonald brothers never fully grasped, that the real product being sold was not the burger itself but a precisely repeatable operating process that could be handed to thousands of independent operators worldwide and still come out identical every time.

## What it does

McDonald's operates and franchises fast food restaurants across more than a hundred countries, selling a standardised menu built around burgers, fries and soft drinks, with the vast majority of its locations run by independent franchisees who pay fees and a share of revenue for the right to operate under the brand.

## How it makes money

McDonald's earns most of its revenue not from selling burgers directly but from franchise fees and rent, since the company itself owns much of the real estate its franchisees operate on, collecting a percentage of each location's sales as rent regardless of how thin that individual restaurant's own profit margin runs. This real-estate-driven model is precisely why analysts sometimes describe McDonald's as a property company that happens to sell food rather than the reverse, a structure that gives the parent company remarkably stable, predictable income even when a given restaurant's food sales fluctuate.

## Products

The Big Mac, introduced in 1967, remains the chain's most recognisable product and is even used informally by The Economist magazine as an international price comparison tool, the "Big Mac Index," precisely because it is sold in a near-identical form across so many countries. McDonald's has also localised its menu significantly by market, offering the McSpicy in several Southeast Asian markets including Malaysia and rice-based meal sets in parts of Asia, reflecting how far the company has adapted its core standardised model to local taste even while keeping its overall operating system consistent.
