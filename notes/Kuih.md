## In one sentence

Kuih is not one dessert but an entire category, hundreds of small, bite-sized Malay and Peranakan snacks and cakes, usually built from rice flour, glutinous rice, coconut and palm sugar, and steamed rather than baked, which is why a Malaysian can grow up eating kuih daily without ever running out of new ones to try.

## The story

Truth be told, nobody agrees on an exact number, but food writers and cultural researchers commonly cite estimates of over 300 to 400 distinct kuih varieties across Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia and Brunei, a scale that makes kuih closer to a whole culinary tradition than a single dish worth one note the way [[Rendang]] or [[Satay]] gets one. The word itself comes from Hokkien, though the kuih tradition as practised in Malaysia draws heavily from Malay, Peranakan (mixed Chinese-Malay heritage) and Indian Muslim culinary influences layered together over centuries.

Kuih has traditionally been strongly tied to specific occasions, weddings, Hari Raya, funerals and everyday afternoon tea, whereby certain kuih are considered appropriate only for certain events, a social coding that has faded somewhat as kuih became available daily at markets and cafes rather than reserved for occasions.

## Why it is the way it is

Under the hood, the two ingredients doing the most work across almost every kuih are rice, in flour or glutinous grain form, and coconut, either as milk, grated flesh or both, which is why kuih textures cluster around a few recognisable families, chewy and glutinous, soft and custardy, or layered and jelly-like, despite the huge variety of individual recipes. Firstly, steaming rather than baking is the dominant cooking method, since it produces the moist, dense texture kuih is known for and does not require an oven, which historically made kuih accessible to home cooks without baking equipment.

Secondly, pandan and gula melaka (palm sugar) show up constantly across different kuih the same way they do in [[Cendol]], functioning as the tradition's two signature flavours, one for aroma and colour, one for a caramel-like sweetness distinct from refined sugar. Thirdly, many kuih are wrapped or steamed in banana leaf, which both shapes the kuih and imparts a subtle earthy aroma no plastic or metal mould replicates.

## The variants

| Name | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| Kuih lapis | Multi-coloured steamed layered rice cake |
| Onde-onde | Glutinous rice balls filled with gula melaka, coated in grated coconut |
| Kuih talam | Two-layer steamed cake, savoury pandan base, sweet coconut top |
| Kuih seri muka | Glutinous rice base topped with a green pandan custard layer |

## Where I met it

In our case, kuih for me means a market visit before 9am, since the good stalls sell out fast and the selection thins visibly by mid-morning, which taught me early that kuih rewards showing up early far more than most other Malaysian food does.
