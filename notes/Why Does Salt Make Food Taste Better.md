## In one sentence

Salt makes food taste better through two separate mechanisms working together, it directly stimulates the tongue's own salt taste receptors, and it suppresses bitterness while enhancing other flavours already present, which is why salted food tastes not just salty but generally more flavourful overall, even in dishes with barely any perceptible salty taste of their own.

## Explain it

Truth be told, "salt makes things taste better" is one of cooking's most repeated pieces of advice, and the mechanism behind it is more interesting than simple seasoning preference. The tongue has dedicated taste receptors specifically tuned to sodium ions, the charged particles salt releases when dissolved in the moisture on food's surface or in saliva, and activating those receptors produces the taste people specifically identify as "salty," one of the five basic taste categories alongside sweet, sour, bitter and umami.

Separately, and more surprisingly, salt genuinely suppresses the perception of bitterness at a neurological level, research has shown sodium ions interfere with how bitter compounds interact with their own separate taste receptors, meaning a food with some bitter notes, many vegetables and some proteins included, tastes measurably less bitter and more balanced once salted, not merely masked by a stronger competing flavour. This in turn means salt's effect on flavour is not purely additive, it is not simply "salty plus whatever was already there," it is actively restructuring how the other flavours already present come through.

## Example / non-example

- Example: a pinch of salt added to a caramel sauce, despite contributing barely any perceptible salty taste itself in small enough quantity, makes the caramel taste noticeably richer and less flatly sweet, consistent with salt's bitterness-suppressing and flavour-balancing effect rather than any salty taste of its own.
- Non-example: sugar added to an overly salty dish does not meaningfully reduce perceived saltiness through the same interference mechanism, since sweet and salty receptors do not interact the same way bitter and salty receptors do, showing this specific suppression effect is not a general rule across all taste pairs.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually change how food gets seasoned in practice? Firstly, this is why professional kitchens season in layers throughout cooking rather than only at the end, salting early lets it interact with a dish's other ingredients and flavours as they develop, rather than sitting only as a surface-level final taste added after everything else is already set.

In addition, this is exactly why bland-tasting vegetables, ones people often describe as bitter or flat, taste dramatically better simply salted before roasting or blanching, the effect many home cooks credit to "better technique" is frequently just salt doing its bitterness-suppressing work rather than any change in cooking method itself.

In our case, the detail that changed how I think about seasoning is that under-salted food is not simply "less flavourful" in some vague sense, it is often specifically more bitter than it needs to be, which reframes salt less as an added flavour and more as a correction applied to flavours already present.
