## In one sentence

Remote work existed at a small scale for decades before 2020, but the COVID-19 pandemic forced a sudden, mass, involuntary experiment that proved far more office work could happen remotely than most companies had previously assumed, and the years since have been a genuine negotiation over how much of that shift is permanent.

## Situation

Before 2020, remote work was a real but minority arrangement, generally offered selectively to specific roles, senior staff, or as a limited perk rather than a default, and many companies that permitted it in principle still culturally treated in-office presence as the expected norm for anyone serious about advancement. The core problem at hand was that most companies had never actually tested whether their work could function remotely at scale, since the arrangement simply had not been forced on them, an untested assumption rather than a settled conclusion.

## Core problem

The one tension this case turns on is that remote work's viability was, for years, a matter of belief and internal politics as much as evidence, companies that were sceptical of it rarely felt pressure to actually test that scepticism, until a global health emergency removed that choice entirely and forced a real, unavoidable trial across nearly every office-based industry simultaneously.

## What happened

Firstly, when lockdowns began in March 2020, companies with virtually no remote work infrastructure had to build it within weeks, video conferencing tools, cloud file access and remote-first communication norms adopted at a pace that would ordinarily have taken years, with [[How Zoom Became Ubiquitous]] covering the specific tool that benefited most directly from this shift.

Secondly, productivity data through 2020 and 2021 surprised many sceptical executives, several studies and internal company reports found output held steady or even improved for many roles, undermining the assumption that in-person presence was necessary for most knowledge work to function well.

Thirdly, as pandemic restrictions eased through 2022 and 2023, companies split into different camps, some embracing permanent remote or hybrid arrangements as a genuine strategic choice, others pushing return-to-office mandates, often citing culture and collaboration concerns that proved harder to measure convincingly than productivity had been.

Lastly, this disagreement produced real friction, several high-profile return-to-office mandates faced employee pushback and some measurable attrition, showing that the pandemic had shifted employee expectations about flexibility in a way many companies underestimated when trying to fully reverse it.

## Lessons

To begin with, an assumption a company has never actually tested is not the same as an assumption a company has confirmed, and remote work's sudden, forced trial revealed how much untested belief, rather than evidence, had shaped office culture for years beforehand.

In addition, once a workforce experiences a genuine alternative to an old norm and finds it workable, reverting fully back to that norm becomes a much harder sell than never having offered the alternative at all, since expectations, once shifted, do not reset just because circumstances technically allow it.
