## In one sentence

Boeing added a new automatic system to the 737 MAX to compensate for engines that changed how the plane handled, did not train pilots on that system to keep costs and certification time down, and two crashes 5 months apart in 2018 and 2019, killing 346 people combined, forced a worldwide grounding of the entire model for almost two years.

## Situation

Boeing launched the 737 MAX in 2017 as an upgraded version of its decades-old 737 line, fitted with larger, more fuel-efficient engines. Those engines sat differently on the airframe and changed the plane's handling enough at high angles of pitch that Boeing added a new automated system, the Manoeuvring Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS), to push the nose down and prevent a stall, without pilots needing to know it existed as a distinct system.

On 29 October 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea, killing all 189 aboard. On 10 March 2019, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed shortly after takeoff, killing all 157 aboard. Both investigations found MCAS had repeatedly forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor, and pilots fought the system without understanding what was overriding their controls. Regulators worldwide grounded the fleet by 13 March 2019, staying grounded until November 2020 in the US, close to 20 months.

## Core problem

The one tension at hand is that Boeing needed the MAX to fly similarly enough to older 737s that airlines would not need to pay for new pilot simulator training, since that cost and delay was a major selling point over the competing Airbus A320neo. MCAS's design, and Boeing's choice not to disclose it prominently to pilots, existed specifically to preserve that "flies just like the old one" promise, at the cost of pilots not knowing a new automated system could seize control of the aircraft.

## What happened

Firstly, MCAS relied on input from only one of two angle-of-attack sensors on the plane at a time, so a single sensor failure, as happened on both crashed flights, could trigger the system based on bad data with no automatic cross-check against the second sensor.

Secondly, internal Boeing communications later released by US congressional investigators showed employees, including test pilots, raising concerns about MCAS and about pressure to minimise training requirements years before either crash, undercutting Boeing's public position that the risk had not been foreseeable.

Thirdly, the FAA had delegated significant portions of the MAX's safety certification to Boeing's own employees, a longstanding practice called Organization Designation Authorization, which investigators found let the MCAS risk pass through review with less independent scrutiny than a fully external process would have applied.

Lastly, Boeing paid over \$2.5 billion in fines and settlements by 2021, including a criminal charge for deceiving regulators, and grounded deliveries cost tens of billions more in lost revenue and airline compensation over the following years.

## Lessons

To begin with, a safety-critical system added specifically to preserve a commercial promise, in this case "no new training needed", is the exact kind of decision that deserves the most scrutiny, not the least, since the incentive to downplay its significance is built into the reason it exists.

In addition, self-certification arrangements between a regulator and the company it regulates work only as well as the regulator's independent oversight of that delegation, and this case is now the standard example cited whenever that arrangement gets challenged elsewhere in aviation or other industries.
