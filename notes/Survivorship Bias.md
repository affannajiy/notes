## In one sentence

Survivorship bias happens when an analysis only looks at the people, companies or things that "survived" some process while ignoring everyone who did not, producing a systematically distorted, overly optimistic picture, and the classic illustration, World War Two bomber armour, shows exactly how counterintuitive the correct conclusion can look once the missing failures are accounted for.

## Explain it

Truth be told, survivorship bias is one of those statistical traps that sounds obvious once explained but is genuinely easy to fall into, because the missing data, by definition, is not sitting in front of you to notice. The clearest historical example comes from statistician Abraham Wald's work during World War Two, military analysts examined returning bomber planes and found bullet holes concentrated in the wings and tail, and initially proposed reinforcing those specific areas with extra armour, since that is where the damage visibly clustered.

Wald pointed out the analysts were only ever looking at planes that made it back, the planes hit in other areas, the engine and cockpit specifically, had simply not survived to be examined at all, meaning the areas showing the least damage on returning planes were actually the most critical to protect, since damage there was fatal rather than survivable. This in turn is the core mechanism of survivorship bias generally, whenever an analysis draws conclusions only from a surviving subset, it systematically misses whatever the surviving group has in common precisely because it lacked the fatal flaw that eliminated everyone else.

## Example / non-example

- Example: studying only successful startups to identify "what makes a company succeed" risks attributing success to traits that failed startups also shared just as often, since the failed companies, the ones that would reveal whether those traits actually mattered, are excluded from the study entirely by virtue of having failed.
- Non-example: a randomised clinical trial that tracks every enrolled participant through to the study's end, including dropouts and those who experienced treatment failure, avoids survivorship bias specifically because it accounts for the full original group rather than analysing only those who completed the study successfully.

## Why it matters

So where does this actually matter beyond a wartime engineering anecdote? Firstly, survivorship bias shows up constantly in business and investing advice, "successful founders all did X" analyses routinely skip the far larger number of founders who also did X and still failed, meaning the trait being praised may have had little or nothing to do with the actual outcome.

In addition, this bias explains part of why old buildings, old furniture and even old cars sometimes seem to have been "built better" than modern equivalents, the surviving old examples are, by definition, the ones sturdy enough to have lasted, while the far larger number of poorly built equivalents from the same era have simply not survived long enough to be counted or compared against.

In our case, the detail worth carrying forward is a simple check, whenever a pattern seems to hold suspiciously well among survivors of some selection process, asking what happened to everyone who did not survive that same process before accepting the pattern as meaningful.
