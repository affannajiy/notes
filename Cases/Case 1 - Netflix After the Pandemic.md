## Situation

Netflix is the streaming company from Los Gatos, California, founded in 1997 by Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph as a DVD-by-post service and now run by co-CEOs Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters. At the end of 2019 it had 167 million paying subscribers. When the world locked down in March 2020, it added 15.8 million subscribers in the first quarter alone and about 26 million in the first half of 2020, which was more than it normally added in a whole year, and it ended 2020 at 204 million. Then in April 2022 it reported its first loss of subscribers in over a decade, 200,000 gone in one quarter, followed by another 970,000 the quarter after, and the share price fell from around \$700 in November 2021 to around \$166 in May 2022, a drop of roughly 75%. Four years later, in January 2026, the same company reported 2025 revenue of \$45.2 billion, net income of \$11.0 billion, an operating margin of 29.5% and 325 million subscribers, and a market value near \$400 billion. So the question at hand is not how the pandemic helped Netflix. It obviously did. The question is how a company that looked finished in mid-2022 ended up bigger than it was at the peak of lockdown.

## Core problem

The pandemic pulled two years of growth into six months and hid the fact that Netflix had run out of easy new customers. The one decision the company had been avoiding since 2019 was whether to charge the estimated 100 million households, about 30 million of them in the US and Canada, who were watching on someone else's password. Netflix had identified the problem internally in 2019, then the lockdown surge arrived and the effort was quietly dropped, because why annoy people when the numbers are going up on their own. The core tension was growth from new subscribers versus money from the subscribers it already had, and the pandemic let the company postpone the choice until the market forced it.

## Analysis

Firstly, the pandemic gain was real but it was borrowed. Everyone who was ever going to sign up for Netflix and had been putting it off did so in the spring of 2020, so 2021 was always going to be thin, and it was, with growth slowing all year even with Squid Game landing in September 2021. Meanwhile Disney+, HBO Max, Peacock and Paramount+ all launched between late 2019 and 2021, so by 2022 the household that used to have Netflix and nothing else had four or five options and a rising bill. This in turn made the password problem urgent. In Layman's terms, Netflix had been giving away roughly a third of its audience for free while its competitors were charging for theirs.

Secondly, the fix was two unpopular decisions made at once. In November 2022 Netflix launched the ad-supported plan at \$6.99 a month, which Reed Hastings had said for years the company would never do. Then from May 2023 it started the password crackdown in the US, whereby your account is tied to one household and anyone outside it has to pay an extra fee or get their own plan. Truth be told, I expected this to blow up in their faces, and so did a lot of people, but the numbers went the other way: from June 2022 to June 2024 Netflix added 57 million subscribers, an average of more than 7 million per quarter, and it crossed 300 million at the end of 2024. Ad revenue was \$1.5 billion in 2025, two and a half times the year before, and is guided to roughly double again to about \$3 billion in 2026.

Thirdly, the company changed what it sells. It stopped reporting subscriber numbers every quarter from 2025 and told Wall Street to look at revenue and margin instead, which is the sign of a business that has grown up: it is no longer a land grab, it is a utility. It bought its way into live events with the Jake Paul versus Mike Tyson fight in November 2024, two NFL games on Christmas Day 2024, and WWE Raw from January 2025 on a ten year deal worth about \$5 billion, because live is the one thing a library of old shows cannot give you and it is what advertisers pay the most for.

## What happened

The turnaround worked and then Netflix tried to do something bigger. In December 2025 it agreed to buy the studio and streaming half of Warner Bros Discovery, meaning the Warner Bros film studio and HBO, for about \$72 billion in cash and shares, or \$82.7 billion including debt. Paramount Skydance, controlled by David Ellison, came back with a hostile all-cash bid for the whole of WBD including the cable channels, and on 26 February 2026 Netflix said it would not match, walked away, and collected a \$2.8 billion break fee for its trouble. Paramount won at \$31 a share, about \$81 billion or \$111 billion with debt, and WBD shareholders approved it in April 2026. So the pandemic winner ended up as the company that could afford to lose an \$80 billion auction and treat the \$2.8 billion consolation as a rounding error. Was this predictable from the situation in 2022? The recovery, yes, once you accept that 100 million free riders were a resource and not a loss. The WBD bid, no, and I still do not know whether losing it was a loss.

## Lessons

- A windfall is not growth. The lockdown subscribers were a one-off, and treating them as a trend is what made the 2022 fall look like a collapse instead of a correction. In our case as students, an A on an easy paper is the same thing.
- The decision you keep postponing is usually the one that fixes it. Netflix knew about password sharing in 2019 and did nothing for three years because the good numbers made it easy not to. The crackdown was the single biggest driver of the recovery.
- Change what you measure when the game changes. Dropping subscriber reporting looked like hiding bad news and was actually the company telling investors that the metric no longer described the business.
- Being able to walk away is worth as much as winning. The \$2.8 billion break fee is more than most media companies earn in a year.
