## Claim / question

Being late is better than never showing up.

## Why this is interesting

"For bet than never is late." - Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales, around 1386

The saying is older than that, since the Roman historian Livy wrote "potius sero quam numquam" about 1,400 years before Chaucer, so people have been arguing about this for at least two millennia and we still do not agree. Truth be told, I picked this one because in Malaysia the argument has a special flavour. We have the phrase "janji Melayu" (a Malay promise, meaning an agreed time that everyone silently understands to be a suggestion), and every group project at UTP has the one person who turns up 40 minutes late with a Milo ais and no apology, and the other one who simply does not turn up at all. Which of the two do I actually prefer? Honestly, it is not as obvious as it sounds.

## The case for

- The work still gets done. If the meeting is at 2pm and you arrive at 2.40pm, the group still has you for the remaining hour, which is a lot more than zero. In a study of 195 employees reporting on over 300 meetings, people admitted to being late about 5% of the time, and yet the meetings still happened and the decisions still got made. Late is a delay; absent is a hole.
- Showing up late is still a signal that you care. You made the trip, you faced the awkward walk to the empty chair, and you took the small hit to your reputation instead of the big one. The person who never comes has decided the thing is not worth their time at all, and there is no coming back from that in a group.
- Late has a fix, absent does not. You can apologise, buy the teh tarik, and stay 20 minutes after to catch up. There is no equivalent gesture for not being there, because you were not there.
- Almost everything worth doing is done late. Submitting the assignment at 11.58pm instead of 8pm is still a submission. Going to the gym at 9pm instead of 6am is still a workout. Calling your mother a week after you said you would is still a call.

## The case against

- Late is a tax on everyone else. Firstly, the same study found that because meetings average 8 people, that 5% individual lateness meant 37% of meetings started late. One person 10 minutes late to a meeting of 8 does not waste 10 minutes, it wastes 80. The absent person costs nothing to the people in the room, which sounds harsh but is true.
- Late is a habit, absent is a decision. Somewhere between 15 and 20% of Americans describe themselves as consistently late, and I would bet the Malaysian figure is higher. A consistently late person is unreliable in a way you have to plan around every single time. A person who says "I cannot make it" once is at least honest, and you can plan without them.
- Late without notice is actually worse than a clear no. If you tell me at 1pm that you cannot come at 2pm, I can move the meeting or start without you. If you say nothing and walk in at 2.40pm, you have taken the choice away from me. So the real comparison is not late versus never, it is "late and silent" versus "never but told you".
- For some things, late is exactly the same as never. A flight, a final exam, a job interview, a funeral. There is no such thing as being late to a plane.

## What the evidence says

- The meeting study (Rogelberg and colleagues, reported by the British Psychological Society) is the best number I found: 5% individual lateness, 8 people per meeting, 37% of meetings starting late, and late starts rated as making the meeting worse by the people in it. So the cost of lateness is real and measured.
- On the other hand, I could not find a single study that measures the cost of non-attendance in the same way, probably because absent people are not in the room to fill in the survey. That gap is interesting in itself: we count the sins of the people who came.
- The proverb has survived 2,000 years across Latin, English and Malay ("lebih baik lambat daripada tidak sama sekali"), which is not evidence that it is true, but it is evidence that it is useful.

## My take

In my opinion, late beats never, but only for things that are still there when you arrive. For a group project, a family dinner, a friend's event, a promise to call, the late person is still a participant and the absent person is a ghost, and I will take the participant every time even if I complain about them. For me, I feel the real villain is not lateness but silence. The person who texts "sorry, running 30 minutes late" at 1.45pm has done nothing wrong in my book. The person who arrives at 2.40pm and says nothing has, and the person who never comes and never texts has done the worst thing of all, which is to make me guess.

To illustrate, in our case at UTP the group member who came late to every meeting but always did their slides was still a better teammate than the one who came to the first meeting on time and then vanished until the presentation. So I land firmly on "late is better than never", with one condition: you tell people.

(Yes, I am the one who is usually 10 minutes late. This note is partly a defence brief.)

## What would change my mind

- A study showing that chronically late people are, on average, also the ones who eventually stop showing up. If lateness is just the early stage of absence, then "late is better" is only true for a while, and the right move is to treat the first late arrival as a warning.
- If the thing at hand is a hard deadline. For flights, exams, interviews and funerals, late is never, and I already concede that.
- If I ever became the person who has to run the 8-person meeting. I suspect my view would flip within a semester.
