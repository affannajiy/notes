## What it does

Truth be told, I picked KFC for the first Monday note because I thought I already knew it.
Everyone in Malaysia has eaten it, and the Colonel is on every second street. Then I read the
annual report and found out I had the whole company wrong, which is a good sign for a first
note.

Kentucky Fried Chicken, or KFC, is the biggest chicken fast-food chain in the world and the
biggest brand inside Yum! Brands, the American parent that also owns Taco Bell, Pizza Hut and
Habit Burger. As of the end of 2025, there were 33,897 KFC restaurants across 149 countries and
territories, and 90% of them sit outside the United States. Together they did \$36.4 billion in
system sales. System sales is the total that customers paid at the till across every store,
regardless of who owns the store, as opposed to the revenue that Yum itself books, which is a
much smaller number that I will get to below.

As for what it sells, it is fried chicken: bone-in Original Recipe with the 11 herbs and spices,
Zinger burgers, wings, tenders, buckets, and then whatever the local market wants on top. The
menu changes a lot from country to country, but the Colonel is the same everywhere.

## How it makes money

Here is the part that surprised me. Yum barely runs any restaurants. About 99% of KFC stores
belong to franchisees, and there are roughly 1,500 of them across the whole Yum group. A
franchisee is a separate business that pays for the right to use the brand. In this case they
pay a one-off fee to open a store, and then a continuing fee of roughly 4 to 6% of sales every
month for the brand, the recipe and the playbook. Above them sit master franchisees, whereby one
company takes on a whole country and sub-franchises under itself. The biggest is Yum China,
which pays 3% of system sales for mainland China.

So in 2025 the KFC Division collected \$1.8 billion in franchise fees and rent, which becomes
\$3.5 billion of revenue once you add the few stores it runs itself, and it turned that into
\$1.5 billion of operating profit. That is a 42% margin. The franchisee, on the other hand,
carries the rent, the staff and the chicken and keeps a much thinner slice: Yum's own
company-run KFCs made a 12% restaurant margin in 2025. Therefore Yum's actual job is the brand,
the menu, the tech (an in-house platform called Byte by Yum!) and picking operators who keep
opening stores. Growth comes from more stores, not from squeezing the existing ones, and 2025
was a record year with 2,986 openings.

## Products and emerging bets

The core menu is Original Recipe bone-in chicken, Hot and Crispy, Zinger, wings, popcorn
chicken and buckets, with local menus on top of it such as rice meals across Asia and porridge
and egg tarts in China.

The big bet right now is boneless. In June 2026 KFC launched what it calls its "next chapter":
tenders, wings and sandwiches dunked in a pantry of 20 or more sauces, a new drinks line called
Kwench (boba refreshers, shakes, sparkling lemonade, iced coffee), a new store design and a
Colonel redrawn by the agency JKR. The rollout starts in the UK and Ireland, then Australia and
the US, with the core of it live in the top 20 markets by the end of 2027. In my opinion this is
the answer to the tender chains eating KFC's lunch at home, which I get to in the next section.

Two more things worth knowing. Firstly, digital is now over 60% of Yum's system sales excluding
Pizza Hut, meaning the order came through an app, a kiosk or a delivery platform, all running on
Byte by Yum!. Secondly, the growth is in emerging markets: 2,986 openings across 105 countries
in 2025, and in Q2 2026 system sales grew 20% in the Middle East, Turkey and North Africa, 16%
in India, and 10% in Asia and Latin America.

## Competitors and edge

At home, KFC is losing. US consumer spend was \$4.34 billion in 2024, down 4%, which put it fifth
among chicken chains behind Chick-fil-A, Popeyes, Raising Cane's and Wingstop according to
Technomic. US system sales then fell another 3% in 2025 and 2% in Q2 2026. The winners all sell
boneless: tenders, sandwiches, wings. To illustrate, Raising Cane's got to third place with a
menu you can count on one hand and stores it runs itself, which is the exact opposite of the
Yum model above.

Everywhere else, KFC is the chicken chain. The US is only 12% of KFC's system sales, whereas
China alone is 26%, with 13,789 stores run by Yum China as of June 2026 and 300-odd more added
every quarter. McDonald's is bigger overall, but nobody else does chicken at this scale across
Asia, Africa and the Middle East.

Why does chicken travel so well? There is no beef problem in India, no pork problem in Muslim
markets, halal is routine, and it is cheap protein everywhere. Add 40 years of local master
franchisees who know their own market and you get a chain that is more Asian than American by
sales.

In our case, Malaysia is the flip side of that same model. KFC here is run by QSR Brands, which
is owned by JCorp, EPF and CVC. In April 2024 it temporarily shut 108 outlets, about a fifth of
the estate, citing the economy, though everyone read it as the Gaza boycott. All of them have
since reopened and QSR's FY2025 revenue rose 31%. So the local operator eats the shock, while
the brand carries a reputation it cannot control from Plano.

## What surprised me

- I had KFC filed as an American chain. By sales it is a Chinese chain with an American origin
  story: China 26%, US 12%.
- Yum gets paid whether or not the store makes money. A 42% operating margin on royalties while
  the operator scrapes 12%.
- Two companies wear one Colonel: a record 2,986 openings worldwide in the same year that US
  sales fell 3%.
- Sanders sold for \$2 million in 1964. The KFC Division now makes \$1.5 billion of operating
  profit a year. He spent his last years telling anyone who asked that the new owners had ruined
  his gravy.
